                                                     2026 RCS FACT SHEET #1
                                             RETIREMENT CONFIDENCE
Just over one in five Americans feel very confident about their ability to have enough
money to live comfortably throughout their retirement years. Workers who say debt is a
problem are, not surprisingly, less confident, while those who have a retirement plan are
more confident.
Confidence in Having a Comfortable Retirement
The 2026 Retirement Confidence Survey (RCS) finds that six in 10 American workers (61
percent) feel confident in their ability to have enough money to live comfortably
throughout their retirement, with 21 percent feeling very confident (Figure 1). This
percentage confident is down from 67 percent in 2025, and it is at its lowest level since
2017.

The level of confidence expressed by those already in retirement is higher than for those
yet to retire. Seventy-three percent of retirees report feeling either very or somewhat
confident about having enough money to live comfortably throughout their retirement
years, which is down from 78 percent in 2025. One-quarter of retirees feel very confident
(25 percent) in 2026 (Figure 2).

Retirement confidence continues to be strongly related to retirement plan
participation, whether in a defined contribution (DC) plan, defined benefit (DB) plan, or
individual retirement account (IRA). Workers reporting they or their spouse have money
in a DC plan or IRA or have benefits in a DB plan from a current or previous employer
are more than twice as likely as those without any of these plans to be at least
somewhat confident (70 percent with a plan vs. 32 percent without a plan) (Figure 3).

Confidence in Other Financial Aspects of Retirement
Nearly seven in 10 workers (69 percent) report feeling very or somewhat confident
about being able to afford basic expenses in retirement, including 28 percent who feel
very confident. Workers’ confidence in their ability to afford basic expenses is higher
than the confidence they report regarding their ability to pay for medical expenses in
retirement. Just over one in three workers (38 percent) are not too or not at all
confident they will have enough money for medical expenses in retirement. A similar
but smaller share is not too or not at all confident that they are doing a good job in
preparing financially for retirement (35 percent) (Figure 4).

Retirees, who are already in that life stage, express higher levels of confidence than
workers in having enough money to take care of expenses (basic and medical) during
retirement. Four out of five retirees (80 percent) feel at least somewhat confident in their
ability to afford basic expenses throughout their retirement years. Seventy-five percent
are very or somewhat confident about having enough money to cover medical
expenses, and 70 percent feel they did a good job preparing financially for retirement
(Figure 4).
Influence of Debt on Confidence
Workers are more likely to say that debt is a problem for them than retirees. Sixty-five
percent of workers say debt is a problem for them, while 35 percent say debt is not a
problem. In contrast, just 40 percent of retirees say that debt is a problem for them.
Workers are almost twice as likely as retirees to say that debt is a major problem (24
percent vs. 13 percent) (Figure 5).

The RCS has consistently found a relationship between debt levels and retirement
confidence. In 2026, 32 percent of workers with a major debt problem say they are very
or somewhat confident about having enough money to live comfortably in retirement,
