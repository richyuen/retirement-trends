1Summary

Most money in American retirement accounts stays where it is until the tax code requires it to move. Tax records, recordkeeper databases and household surveys agree on that, and it has held for two decades. What has changed is how many owners are old enough to be covered by the rules, where the rules draw the line, and how much money leaves employer plans as rollovers.

  1. Withdrawals follow the required minimum distribution (RMD) rules. In a typical year about one in five traditional IRA investors between 60 and the RMD age takes money out. At RMD age about four in five do. When Congress waived RMDs in 2009 and 2020, that share fell to 47% and 60%.
  2. Incidence is rising mainly because the owner population is older. 33% of traditional IRA households took a withdrawal in tax year 2024, the highest in ICI's survey, against 18–22% in 2006–2014 outside the 2009 waiver year. People 70 or older were 18% of IRA holders in IRS data for 2012 and 22% in 2023, a shift that more than accounts for the rise in the IRS measure.
  3. Raising the RMD age moved the cliff. Among IRA holders aged 70 to 74, the IRS counted 86 withdrawers per 100 year-end holders in 2019, 69 in 2021 and 2022 (RMD age 72) and 62 in 2023 (RMD age 73).
  4. Withdrawal rates are low and anchored on the RMD. At RMD age the median withdrawal is 5–6% of the balance at every balance size. In normal years 52–58% of RMD-age withdrawers take exactly the required amount and 33–39% take more. Traditional IRA withdrawals have run at about 4–5% of prior-year assets in almost every year since 2000. Counting only households that withdraw, the SCF puts the rate at 5–7% of the balance, with no fall in the 2009 waiver year.
  5. Full cashouts are common by headcount and small in dollars. About a quarter of people who leave an employer plan at 60 or older cash out entirely (21–29% across Vanguard cohorts from 2004 to 2020), but they hold 3–6% of the assets. None of the IRA sources used here reports account closures.
  6. Rollovers are the largest flow, and most of the dollars now come from people of retirement age. $682 billion was rolled into IRAs in 2023, three times the $226 billion of 2000. People 60 or older supplied 41% of rollover dollars in 2001, 54% in 2010 and 66% in 2023.
  7. More retirement-age money is staying in the plan. Five years after leaving, the share of assets still in the plan rose from 18% for people who left in 2007 to 32% for those who left in 2016, while the share rolled to IRAs fell from 72% to 56%. Few of those who stay set up installment payments. Government and survey data point the same way: a year after separating, 67–71% of federal TSP leavers still hold a balance, against 57–61% in 2015–2016 (Section 11).
  8. Surveys see very different amounts of this. The SCF, SIPP and HRS capture 94–99% of the retirement plan income in tax records for people 65 or older. The CPS captured 47% in 2017, after its 2014 redesign.
  9. The household microdata confirm the pattern. Tabulated for this report, the SCF and CPS show the same step at the RMD age, the same waiver dips (at ages 70 to 74, down 43% in the SCF for 2009 and 30% in the CPS for 2020, against 32% in IRS data for 2020) and the same drop at 70 to 74 once the RMD age passed 72. For people 60 or older in 2021, the SCF recorded withdrawal dollars equal to 92% of the IRS total and the CPS 53%.
  10. Laws that change defaults work; incentives and permissions have done little so far. Automatic enrollment lifts participation to 85–95% where plans adopt it, and the later RMD ages and the TSP’s 2019 withdrawal options kept money in accounts longer. Yet automatic enrollment spread from 4% to 31% of 401(k) plans between 2010 and 2024 while the employer survey’s share of private workers in a plan stayed at 51–53% (tax records show a rise in workers deferring, from 35% to 43% in 2008–2020), and startup credits reach about 5% of eligible firms (Section 12).
  11. Account withdrawals are replacing DB pensions as a source of retirement income. In the SCF, withdrawals rose from 5.0% to 8.0% of the income of households 65 or older between 2003 and 2021, and the share drawing on an account from 21% to 28%. Earnings have also grown, while Social Security remains about half of income for the median older household. Older households spend nearly all of their income (Section 13).
  12. Retirement horizons are back where they were before COVID, not longer. Life expectancy at 65 was 19.7 years in 2024, 0.07 above 2019 after a drop of more than a year. For someone turning 65 in 2025 the Trustees expect 19.1 more years for men and 21.7 for women, 0.1–0.2 years less than projected before COVID (Section 14).

Two measures run through the report. Incidence is the share of owners, households or accounts with a withdrawal in a year. Rate is the amount withdrawn as a share of the balance. They behave differently: incidence jumps at the RMD age, while rates rise slowly with age. A rate can also be averaged two ways. The unweighted average of individual rates is higher than total dollars withdrawn divided by total balances, because small accounts are often emptied. One tax-data study puts the average person's withdrawal at about 13% of the balance among people subject to RMDs in a normal year. On a dollar-weighted basis, people 70 or older withdraw about 6–8% (Figure 6).

The tax system records every distribution on Form 1099-R. A code on the form marks direct rollovers and a checkbox marks total distributions, but none of the IRS tabulations used here publish totals by code. The split between partial and full withdrawals therefore has to come from recordkeepers and surveys.

"In retirement" here means IRA owners from about 60 onward and plan participants who leave an employer at 60 or older. Hardship withdrawals and cash-outs by younger job changers are outside the scope except as context.

3The rules behind the numbers

Two ages organize the data. Before 59½, most withdrawals carry a 10% additional tax. From the RMD age, a minimum must come out every year: the prior year-end balance divided by a life-expectancy divisor. The two waivers and the two age increases show up as breaks in the series that follow.

4IRA withdrawals: who takes them

Four sources measure the share of traditional IRA owners who take a withdrawal in a year, each with a different unit. ICI's survey counts households that still own an IRA. The ICI and EBRI databases count investors at the firms that supply data, so a withdrawal taken at another firm is missed. The IRS counts every taxpayer with a reported distribution, including people who emptied an account, against the number of holders at year-end. The levels differ for those reasons. Where the series overlap they move together: ICI's survey and database rise to 2019 and fall in both waiver years, and IRS data, charted from 2012, show the 2020 fall.

In ICI's survey the share was 18–22% in tax years 2006 to 2014 (15% in 2009), 25–27% in 2015 to 2019, 23% in 2020 and 29–33% in 2021 to 2024. The step in 2015 coincides with the survey's move from telephone to an online panel in 2016, so part of it is method. ICI's database rises from 17.6% of investors in 2007 to 25.1% in 2019 and stood at 23.1% in 2023. EBRI's annual updates, which end with 2017 data, show 25–28% of traditional IRA owners in 2013 to 2017. The IRS ratio rises from 35 per 100 holders in 2012 to 40 in 2019 and 2023.

An older owner population accounts for most of the rise. Households at RMD age were 16–17% of traditional IRA households in ICI's 2007 to 2014 surveys and 23–24% in 2022 to 2025. In IRS data, people 70 or older were 17.5% of all IRA holders in 2012 and 22.4% in 2023, and they took 47% of IRA withdrawal dollars in 2012 and 57% in 2023. Holding each age group's 2012 incidence fixed, the shift in age mix alone would have added about 4 points to the IRS ratio for all IRA types, more than the 3-point rise recorded (computed).

The step at the RMD age

Below 60, 5–8.5% of investors withdraw in a year. Between 60 and the RMD age the share has held between 17% and 22% since 2007. At RMD age it is 78–85% in normal years. The figure is below 100% partly because an investor can satisfy the RMD from an IRA at another firm. ICI's household survey, which sees all of a household's IRAs, finds 80–84% of RMD-age households withdrawing in tax years 2015 to 2024 outside the waiver year, against 5–8% of households younger than 59.

The two waivers show what the rule itself does. In the ICI database the share of RMD-age investors withdrawing fell to 46.9% in 2009 and 59.6% in 2020. In IRS data, 3.2 million of 8.1 million traditional IRA owners aged 70½ or older took nothing in 2009. A study of tax records finds that 35% of those who had withdrawn in 2008 stopped in 2009, and TIAA's records give 35.5%. National taxable IRA distributions on Form 1040 fell 16.6% in 2009 and 12.6% in 2020. The same tax-data study estimates that about half of the people subject to RMDs would withdraw less if they could (41–52% across versions of the study). It also finds that roughly a fifth kept taking the suspended amount in 2009, which suggests the RMD works as a default as well as a constraint.

The cliff moved when the age moved

From 2012 to 2019 the age profile barely moved: 24–25 withdrawers per 100 holders at ages 60 to 64, 32–35 at 65 to 69, 85–88 at 70 to 74 and close to 100 above 75. The 2020 waiver cut the three oldest groups to 59, 74 and 79. With the RMD age at 72, the 70-to-74 group settled at 69 in 2021 and 2022, and with the age at 73 it fell to 62 in 2023. The groups above 75 returned to their earlier level.

Single-year-of-age data show the same shift. Among IRA owners with at least $10,000, 85.3% of 71-year-olds took a distribution in 2019. In 2021, with the RMD age at 72, 31.7% of 71-year-olds and 82.4% of 72-year-olds did. In Fidelity's 401(k) data for 2023, 20% of retirees aged 70 to 72 made withdrawals, against 94% of those 73 or older. The low-incidence years now extend about two and a half years further into retirement than they did before 2020.

Are people in their 60s withdrawing more often?

The sources disagree. In IRS data the ratio at ages 65 to 69 rose from 33 per 100 holders in 2012 to 35 in 2019 and 38 in 2023. At ages 60 to 64 it was 24 in both 2012 and 2021 and reached 28 in 2023. The late rise coincides with a jump in the IRS's count of Roth IRA withdrawers, from 1.0 million in 2021 to 2.5 million in 2023. The IRS revised its methods from tax year 2022 and describes 2022–2023 as a new series, so part of the rise may come from the change in method (see data notes). ICI's survey shows no clear trend for households aged 59 to 69: 21–27% in tax years 2015 to 2022. ICI's database shows none either: 24.7% of investors aged 65 to 69 withdrew in 2018 and 23.2% in 2023. Whether voluntary withdrawals before the RMD age are becoming more common is not settled.

Roth IRAs follow a different pattern because they have no lifetime RMDs. About 3–4% of Roth IRA investors withdraw in a year, and 6–7% of those 70 or older. In ICI's survey, 6% of Roth IRA households withdrew in tax year 2024.

Dollar amounts have grown. In ICI's survey the median withdrawal was $6,500 to $10,000 in tax years 2010 to 2016 and $12,500 to $15,000 in 2021 to 2024, and the share of withdrawals of $20,000 or more rose from 20–34% to 41–44%. The median share of the balance withdrawn did not rise: it was 6–9% in tax years 2007 to 2014 and 5–6% from 2015 on. That is consistent with larger balances rather than faster drawdown.

Rates by age and balance

At 73 or older the median is between 5.0% and 6.0% in all nine balance bands. Before the RMD age it depends on the balance: 68–90% for accounts under $5,000 and about 5% for accounts of $200,000 or more. EBRI's 2013 data show the same thing. The median withdrawal rate was 10.6% at ages 60 to 64, 6.9% at 65 to 70 and 4.1% at 71 to 79, where the middle half of owners fell between 3.5% and 6.0%. By balance, the median was 87% for accounts under $5,000 and 4.2% for accounts of $250,000 or more.

On a dollar-weighted basis, people 70 or older withdrew 6.7–8.0% of prior-year balances in each year from 2012 to 2019, 4.6% in the 2020 waiver year and 5.9–6.1% in 2021 to 2023. The lower level since 2021 is consistent with the higher RMD age and the 2022 tables, although no study isolates those causes. People aged 60 to 69 have withdrawn about 3% a year since 2014. These rates divide by the balances of all holders, including the majority who took nothing; Section 10 compares them with rates among withdrawers only. For traditional IRAs as a whole, withdrawals were between 3.5% and 5.3% of prior year-end assets in every year from 2001 to 2022 except 2010, when the figure was 6.2%. The IRS attributes the 2010 jump to taxpayers with incomes of $100,000 or more. That group became eligible to convert to Roth IRAs that year and $65 billion was converted, so conversions were probably counted in the withdrawal total (see data notes).

For someone who takes only the RMD, the schedule is the table: 3.8% at 73, 5.0% at 80, 6.3% at 85 and 8.2% at 90. Since most RMD-age owners take the RMD and no more, the statutory table is in effect the drawdown schedule for most owners once RMDs apply.

The RMD and nothing more

The share taking more than the RMD has stayed between 33% and 39% for more than a decade, including the 2020 waiver year. What changed in 2020 was that fewer people took exactly the table amount (43.6%, against 57.2% in 2018) and more took less (17.9%, against 4.1%). Other sources use different bases and reach the same conclusion:

  • EBRI finds that about a quarter of traditional IRA owners aged 71 or older withdrew more than their RMD in a given year from 2011 to 2017 (23–27% where a figure is published). Over six years, 57% took only the RMD every year and 11% exceeded it every year.
  • A J.P. Morgan and EBRI study of 2013–2018 data finds that about 84% of people subject to RMDs took only the RMD, and about 80% of those below RMD age took nothing.
  • In the Health and Retirement Study, 61–68% of IRA households aged 71 or older withdrew no more than the RMD in each wave from 2002 to 2016.
  • Among Vanguard's RMD-age IRA clients in 2024, 69% withdrew at least the RMD, 24% withdrew less and 6.7% withdrew nothing.

A withdrawal is not the same as spending. Among retired households that withdrew in tax year 2024, 37% used the money for living expenses and 44% reinvested or saved at least part of it in another account. In the previous year's survey, nine in ten of those who reinvested had taken an RMD-based amount.

6Full cashouts

The clearest evidence on full cashouts comes from employer plans, where recordkeepers can follow each person who leaves. The result depends on whether people or dollars are counted.

Across every Vanguard cohort from 2004 to 2020, between 21% and 29% of people who left at 60 or older took the whole balance in cash, and those accounts held 3–6% of the assets. Other recordkeepers report the same pattern (Table 5). Cash-outs are concentrated in small balances. In Health and Retirement Study data for 2008 and 2010, 46% of people leaving with less than $5,000 withdrew the money, against 10% of those with $100,000 or more.

The headcount share has changed little in recordkeeper data. Five years after leaving it was 26% for both 2007 and 2016 leavers. One year after leaving it rose from 21% to 26% between the 2011 and 2020 cohorts (Figure 8). Vanguard's newer series, which measures at the end of the retirement year, shows 14–18% for the 2021 to 2024 cohorts. A separate Vanguard study that followed 70,000 long-tenured workers who left at 60 or older finds that 23% cashed out entirely within a year, 27% touched nothing for five years and half took withdrawals, RMDs included. Survey data over a longer span point up. Among workers who entered the Health and Retirement Study at 51 to 56 and left a job within eight years, the share cashing out a DC plan rose from 13.6% for the 1992 cohort to 24.0% for the 2004 cohort. That sample includes separations before retirement age, and its authors note that large cash-outs cluster at 59 to 60, when the early-withdrawal tax ends.

At these ages a cash-out may be the start of retirement spending rather than leakage in the usual sense. None of the sources separates the two.

IRAs: indirect evidence only

None of the ICI, EBRI or IRS series reports how many IRAs are emptied each year. ICI's survey interviews only households that still own an IRA, and neither database splits withdrawals into partial and total. The indirect evidence points to two patterns. Small accounts are largely emptied when they are tapped before the RMD age, and closures become more common once RMDs begin and at advanced ages.

  • Withdrawals from accounts under $5,000 take most of the balance when the owner is below RMD age: a median of 90% at ages 60 to 64 and 68% at 70 to 72 (Figure 5).
  • In EBRI's 2011 data, 21.9% of traditional IRAs that received a rollover also had a withdrawal in the same year, and in 29.5% of those cases the withdrawal was at least as large as the rollover.
  • IRS data count slightly more withdrawers than year-end holders at 80 or older (Figure 4), which means some of them ended the year with no balance.
  • Household IRA ownership falls at advanced ages. In the 2002 Health and Retirement Study, 42% of households aged 55 to 64 owned an IRA, 33% at 75 to 79 and 9% at 85 to 89. Part of that gap reflects earlier generations who never had IRAs.
  • A Joint Committee on Taxation staff study of tax records finds that people first subject to RMDs are 28% more likely to close their accounts than people aged 60 to 70½.

7Employer plans after retirement

What retirement-age participants do with a 401(k)-type balance has shifted more than what they do with an IRA. The shift is toward leaving money in the plan.

Retention decays quickly, but the level has risen over a decade. On a like-for-like basis (Figure 8), the share of assets still in the plan five years after leaving rose from 18% to 32% between Vanguard's 2007 and 2016 cohorts. At T. Rowe Price, 45% of the assets of participants 65 or older were still in the plan a year after separation in 2012 and 61% in 2018. At Alight, 69% of people who left at 60 or older in 2008 took money out within the year, against 61% of those who left in 2017. The newest Vanguard cohorts do not show further gains: 53–54% of 2021 and 2022 retirees remained at the end of the retirement year, and 49% of 2024 retirees.

The federal Thrift Savings Plan shows a similar change, though the two measures are not strictly comparable. Of participants who separated in 2012, about half still had their account at the end of 2013 (49% had taken no action and 2% only a partial withdrawal). Another 41% had withdrawn in full, mostly as a cash lump sum, and 8% had received a cash-out. After the plan added withdrawal options in 2019, its own metric has shown 68–73% of separated FERS participants still holding a balance a year later.

Plan design moved with it, but few use the new options

Vanguard finds that people in plans allowing partial withdrawals are more likely to stay: 38% of assets remained after five years in such plans, against 28% elsewhere, for the 2016 cohort. Those plans are also larger and have higher balances, so the comparison is not a clean test. Use of the options is limited. Only 3–4% of each Vanguard cohort was taking installments from the plan. Of 2021 retirees still in a flexible plan three years on, 13% were taking installments and 18% had taken partial withdrawals. Alight reports that 3–6% of people who left after 60 chose installments in a year, and T. Rowe Price that 11% of separated participants aged 59½ or older receive them. Rising retention is mostly money left untouched.

That fits the IRA evidence. At T. Rowe Price, 76–83% of separated participants aged 59½ to 72 took no withdrawal in a twelve-month period in each year from 2018 to 2024. For people who keep balances in tax-deferred accounts, the RMD age is the effective start date for withdrawals.

Annuities play almost no part. A 2016 GAO review found that about two-thirds of the plans it covered offered no withdrawal option and about three-quarters offered no annuity. At TIAA, where annuities are built into the plan, the share of first-time income recipients choosing a life annuity fell from 61% in 2000 to 18% in 2018, while the share whose first payment was an RMD rose from 10% to 52%. In the Thrift Savings Plan, 1% of 2012 separators bought an annuity.

Private DC plans now pay out more than they take in

Private DC plans disbursed $784 billion in 2023 against $768 billion of contributions. Contributions exceeded disbursements in every year from 2001 to 2012. The reverse has held in 2013 to 2015 and in every year since 2017. The outflow rate has not risen: disbursements were 7–10% of plan assets in every year from 2000 to 2023, so the dollar growth tracks asset growth. The Bureau of Economic Analysis measure for all DC plans reached $874 billion in 2023 and $1,038 billion in 2025. Among participants of all ages, 3–4% took any withdrawal from a plan in each year from 2008 to 2022.

8Rollovers

In dollars, the rollover is the main way money leaves an employer plan. It is also the main way money enters an IRA.

Rollovers into IRAs were $215 billion in 2004, $300 billion in 2010, $618 billion in 2020, a peak of $740 billion in 2021, and $682 billion in 2023. Contributions were $89 billion in 2023, so rollovers were more than seven times contributions. The number of taxpayers making a rollover grew much less than the dollars, from 4.3 million in 2010 to 5.9 million in 2023, and the average rollover rose from $69,000 to $117,000. For traditional IRAs alone, rollovers made up 89% to 96.5% of new money in every year from 1996 to 2018.

Rollovers have become a retirement-age event

People 60 or older supplied 40.6% of rollover dollars in 2001, 53.5% in 2010 and 66.1% in 2023. People 55 or older supplied 80% in 2023. The shift is in dollars more than in people: taxpayers 60 or older were 36% of those making a rollover in 2010 and 34% in 2023, but the average rollover at ages 60 to 64 rose from $109,000 to $232,000. ICI's survey points the same way. Six in ten traditional IRA households hold rollover money. Within the online panel used since 2016, the share naming retirement as a reason for the rollover rose from 34–36% in the 2016 to 2020 surveys to 43–46% in 2022 to 2025. A job change is still named by about 70%.

Two things can be true together. A growing share of rollover dollars comes from retirees, and a shrinking share of retirees' plan dollars is rolled over. In Vanguard's data the share of assets rolled to an IRA within five years fell from 72% to 56% between the 2007 and 2016 cohorts (Figure 8). Survey data on lump sums show the same age pattern in a different form: in 2012, 56% of recipients aged 61 to 64 put the entire distribution into tax-qualified savings, against 42% of those 65 or older. The share doing so across all ages rose from 19% in 1993 to 35% in 1998 and was 43–45% in 2003, 2006 and 2012.

9What household surveys add

Surveys add three things the administrative data lack: ownership across the whole population, what people do with the money, and how total assets change through retirement. They also differ sharply in how much withdrawal activity they record.

More households reach old age with an account. In the Survey of Consumer Finances, 22.8% of households headed by someone 65 or older held a retirement account in 1992, 40.8% in 2007 and 47% in 2022. In the Survey of Income and Program Participation, the share of households aged 80 or older with a personal retirement account rose from 6.8% in 1997 to 34.3% in 2010. A growing pool of account holders past the RMD age raises overall withdrawal incidence even if nobody changes behavior.

The CPS is the outlier. Before 2014 it counted retirement account money only when it arrived as a regular payment. For 2012, 37% of people 65 or older reported retirement income in the survey, against 61% in linked IRS records. For people whose only distributions came from IRAs, 81% of those with income in the records reported none. The 2014 redesign raised the number of people reporting IRA or 401(k) income more than fivefold, and a 2019 processing change raised it by another 74%. A gap remains: for 2015 the redesigned CPS showed 47% of people 65 or older with pension or retirement account income, against 69% in administrative records. Any CPS series that spans those changes has two breaks in it.

The SCF has its own break. The Federal Reserve moved the IRA withdrawal question in 2004, and the share of IRA households reporting a withdrawal went from 0.5% in 1998 and 1.7% in 2001 to 17.4% in 2004 and 19.9% in 2007. For 2006, total IRA withdrawals were $6.4 billion in the CPS, $72 billion in ICI's survey, $95 billion in the SCF and $125 billion on tax returns. SCF withdrawal data are usable from 2004, and the Federal Reserve publishes no regular table from them. Section 10 tabulates them.

The age pattern matches the administrative data. In SIPP, about 20–22% of account owners aged 65 to 69 took a distribution in 1998, 2006 and 2009. Among owners 70 or older the share was 70–72% in 1998 and 2006, and 59% in 2009 when RMDs were waived. In the Health and Retirement Study, which asks about a two-year window, 22% of IRA households withdraw at 61, 41% at 69 and 77% at 71. Poterba, Venti and Wise put average withdrawals at about 2% of balances a year before 70 and about 5% after.

Total assets fall slowly. In 2019, 28.7% of people 65 or older lived in a household with IRA withdrawals, with a median of $8,945. In SIPP for 2022, 16% of adults 65 or older had IRA income and 10% had income from a 401(k)-type plan. After 17 to 18 years of retirement, households still held about 80% of their pre-retirement non-housing assets, and more than a third had more than they started with. EBRI finds a median decline over 18 years of 4% for retirees with pensions and 34% for those without.

10Checking against the microdata

For this edition the public microdata of two household surveys were tabulated directly, to test the patterns above against sources that count people rather than accounts. The SCF files cover the 2004 to 2022 waves. Each wave asks about withdrawals in the previous calendar year, so the withdrawal years run from 2003 to 2021. The CPS ASEC files cover income years 2009 to 2025. Neither survey matches the IRS in level, because each counts a different unit. The SCF counts households, grouped by the age of the household head at the interview, which comes about a year after the withdrawals it asks about. Its incidence is withdrawing households per 100 households holding the account. The CPS cannot identify account holders, so its measure is the share of all people in an age group who received a withdrawal. The comparison below therefore rests on age patterns and on changes over time, not on levels.

The SCF gives the clearest survey reading of the first waiver. Among IRA-holding households aged 70 to 74, 43.7 per 100 withdrew in 2009, against 76.6 in 2006 and 69.7 in 2012, with standard errors of 3 to 4.5 points. The 2020 waiver falls between SCF waves, but the CPS shows it: 23.1% of all people aged 70 to 74 received a withdrawal in 2019 and 16.1% in 2020, a fall eight times its standard error. With Census’s weights for pandemic nonresponse the figures are 22.3% and 15.8%.

Both surveys also show the move in the RMD age. In the SCF, incidence at 70 to 74 was 70.5 per 100 IRA households in 2018 and 62.5 in 2021, while at 75 to 79 it stayed near 90. In the CPS, receipt at 70 to 74 has held at 17–19% since 2021, below the 22–23% of 2018 and 2019. That gap is well outside sampling error: every year from 2022 to 2025 is 4.6–6.0 points below 2019, with standard errors under 1 point. Receipt at 75 to 79 rose from 22.6% in 2018 to 25.0% in 2025, a rise that is only marginally significant. That is the same shift Figure 4 shows in tax data.

The SIPP shows the move by single year of age. At 72, 64–67 per 100 IRA holders withdrew in 2020 to 2022, that cohort’s first RMD year under the SECURE Act. With the first RMD moved to 73, the figure fell to 37 in 2023 and 30 in 2024, while 73-year-olds were at 73–76 in 2021 to 2023 and 67 in 2024.

Three decades of SIPP

The SIPP can be followed back to 1996 for IRAs and 401(k)-type accounts combined, in three pieces with different questions. The 1996, 2001, 2004 and 2008 panels asked each month about “distributions from IRAs, Keogh and 401k plans” and once a year about balances, which gives withdrawals over the 12 months before each balance date for 1996–2004 and 2008–2010. The redesigned survey dropped that question. From 2014 to 2019 it asked only whether a person received “any lump sum or regular distribution payments from a retirement plan”, a question that also covers traditional pensions and was reworded in 2017. Account-specific questions return in the 2021 file. The years 2005–2007 and 2011–2013 have no usable items, and 2020 is left out because only retired owners were asked.

At 75 or older, 67–80 per 100 owners withdrew in 1996 to 2004 and 74–86 in 2021 to 2024. The 2008 panel reads lower, 55–67, partly because its windows overlap the 2009 waiver: the point labelled 2008 runs from late 2008 through most of 2009. At 70 to 74 the share fell from 62–71 in 1996 to 2000 to 51–59 in 2021 to 2024. Until 2019 the first RMD came at 70½, so most of that age band was already required to draw; since then it has come at 72, and since 2023 at 73.

Before RMD age the newer files find more withdrawers: 13–17 per 100 at 60 to 64 in 2021 to 2024 against 10–15 in 1996 to 2010, and 25–28 at 65 to 69 against 18–22. Part of that rise is probably the questions. The older panels asked about distributions, the newer files about “income or withdrawals”, and at 55 to 59 the newer files find 6–8 per 100 against about 1, a gap too large to be real (inferred). The pooled question of 2014 to 2019 is not comparable with either side. Its readings jumped 8–12 points at every age when it was reworded in 2017, while leaving out people with a traditional pension lowers them by at most 6 points.

Below that cut, the rate at 70 or older was 11.1% in 1996, 7–10% in 1997 to 2004, 5–7% in 2008 to 2010 and 6.1–7.6% in 2021 to 2024. Including every owner, the 2021–2023 rate at 70 or older is 3.7–4.1%, as above. The older levels should not be read as a decline. Those panels recorded only about half of the IRA balances in ICI’s totals, against nearly all of them since 2017, and their coverage of DC balances doubled between 1996 and 2005. A smaller denominator raises the rate, so the older rates are probably overstated and the fall within 1996 to 2010 partly reflects better coverage (inferred). What holds in every era is the age pattern: rates under 4% before 70 and 5–16% from 75.

The same step at the RMD age

In 2018, a normal year, the SCF and IRS age profiles have the same shape: 19 and 25 per 100 IRA holders at 60 to 64, 27 and 34 at 65 to 69, 71 and 86 at 70 to 74, and 90–95 and 97–101 at 75 or older. The SCF runs 5 to 15 points lower at every age, most at 70 to 74. Two differences in method plausibly explain much of the gap, though it has not been measured. SCF ages are taken about a year after the withdrawal, which moves some people who were not yet 70½ into the 70-to-74 group. And the IRS ratio divides by year-end holders, so people who emptied an account count as withdrawers but not as holders, which is why it can pass 100.

The CPS profile peaks at 70 to 79 and drops at 80 or older. That reflects ownership, not behavior: in the 2019 SCF, 47% of households aged 70 to 74 held a retirement account, 40% at 75 to 79 and 35% at 80 or older. Ownership among the oldest is rising, and the CPS picks that up. Households 80 or older with an account went from 20% in the 2004 SCF to 39% in 2022, and CPS receipt at 80 or older rose from 15.6% in 2017 to 20.4% in 2025, while IRS incidence per holder at those ages held near 100. SCF incidence per holder at older ages rose too, from 74 per 100 IRA households at 75 to 79 and 67 at 80 or older in 2003 to 81–90 and 84–95 in 2012 to 2021. The IRS series is not consistent before 2012, so that rise cannot be checked against tax data.

How much is withdrawn, and how much each survey sees

The SCF confirms that withdrawals are small relative to balances. Households 60 or older withdrew 2.2–3.1% of their retirement account balances in each year measured, the low point being 2009. By age, the 2018 rates were 0.9% at 60 to 64, 1.6% at 65 to 69, 3.7% at 70 to 74, 5.7% at 75 to 79 and 6.5% at 80 or older. IRS rates for IRAs alone are higher at every age (2.6%, 3.1%, 5.5%, 6.6% and 9.1%). That is expected, because SCF balances include employer-plan accounts, which are drawn on far less, and are measured at the interview rather than at the end of the prior year. Among SCF households 60 or older, 13–25 per 100 holders of employer-plan accounts withdrew in a year, against 36–55 per 100 IRA holders. That fits retirees rolling plan money into IRAs before drawing on it (Section 8), though the survey cannot show the sequence.

The surveys differ most in how much they capture (Table 8). For 2021, SCF households headed by someone 60 or older reported $373 billion of withdrawals from IRAs and employer-plan accounts, 92% of the $405 billion the IRS recorded from IRAs alone for taxpayers 60 or older. The CPS recorded $214 billion, 53% of the IRS figure, even though its question also covers employer plans. That is close to the 47% capture rate for 2017 in Figure 14 (computed).

Withdrawal rates: all owners against withdrawers only

A withdrawal rate can divide withdrawals by the balances of everyone who holds an account, or only by the balances of those who withdrew. The first mixes how many people withdraw with how much they take. The second isolates how much. The SCF is the only source here that gives both on the same basis over time, because it records each household’s withdrawals and balance together. In this subsection a rate is the prior year’s withdrawals divided by the balance at the interview, for all retirement accounts.

Across all households 60 or older that hold an account, withdrawals were 2.2–3.1% of balances in every year measured. Among households that withdrew, they were 5.2–6.9% on a dollar-weighted basis, and the median withdrawing household took 5.7–7.5% of its balance. The two measures part in the waiver year. In 2009 the all-owner rate fell from 3.0% to 2.2% because fewer households withdrew, while the withdrawers-only rate held at 6.8% (6.9% in 2006). At ages 70 to 74 the all-owner rate fell from 4.5% to 2.8%, and the withdrawers-only rate was 5.0% in 2006 and 5.2% in 2009. The waiver changed who withdrew more than how much those who withdrew took. ICI’s survey shows the same: the median withdrawal was 8% of the balance in tax years 2008 and 2009.

Over the whole period the withdrawers-only rate edged down, from 6.6–6.9% in 2003 to 2009 to 5.2–6.1% in 2012 to 2021, while the all-owner rate did not trend. Over the same years the share of holders who withdrew rose from about 44 to 47–49 per 100, so smaller withdrawals per withdrawer were roughly offset by more withdrawers (inferred).

The SIPP, tabulated for this report for 2021 to 2023, gives the same split for individuals. Across all owners 60 or older, IRA and employer-plan withdrawals were 2.3–2.7% of balances: 1.2–1.7% at 60 to 69 and 3.7–4.1% at 70 or older. Among those who withdrew they were 5.4–5.7% dollar-weighted, with a median of 5.1–5.6%. In 2020, among retired IRA owners, incidence at 75 or older was 10–20 points lower than in 2021–2023, while the median rate among withdrawers at 80 or older did not change, the same pattern as the SCF in 2009. Few withdrawers empty an account: 1–2% of IRA withdrawers 60 or older ended the year with a zero balance.

What the microdata cannot add. Neither survey separates the three exits. The CPS asks about income received, so rollovers are presumably excluded, but it has no account holders to divide by. Both surveys confirm who withdraws and when; neither can test the cashout and rollover findings in Sections 6 to 8.

11Staying in the plan: evidence beyond recordkeepers

Section 7 shows more retirement-age money staying in employer plans, but nearly all of that evidence comes from recordkeepers, whose clients may not be typical. Two administrative sources and one household survey point the same way. None of them gives a rate for older leavers like Vanguard’s, so each is a proxy.

Retention held at 64–65% from early fiscal 2018 to mid-2020. It rose to 68–73% in fiscal 2021 and early 2022, after the TSP Modernization Act gave separated participants more flexible withdrawal options in September 2019, and has since settled at 67–71%. The measure counts anyone with a balance above zero, so people taking installments or a partial withdrawal count as staying, and FRTIB publishes no age split. Under the old rules, 49% of people who separated in 2012 had closed their accounts by the end of 2013: 41% through a full withdrawal and 8% through an automatic cash-out of a small balance.

Within each consistent stretch of Form 5500 data, the separated share rose: from 15.2% to 18.4% in 1999–2004, from 16.5% to 17.5% in 2005–2008 and from 19.1% to 22.2% in 2014–2023. In the TSP, separated accounts went from 21% of all accounts at the end of 2011 to 35% in 2025. Both are stocks rather than rates for leavers. They rise when more leavers keep money in the plan, but also when automatic enrollment creates small accounts that are later left behind, and neither has an age split.

For households aged 60 to 74 who are not working, the share was 16–19% in 2004 and 2007 and 26–29% in 2019 and 2022; across all households it went from 15–17% to 22–26%. 2019 is high in most groups, so the safer comparison is 2004–2007 against 2019–2022. The measure understates staying among leavers, because IRAs also hold money that was never in a plan. By dollars, former employers’ plans held 14–21% of these households’ combined plan and IRA balances in 2004–2013 and 19–27% in 2016–2022, but those estimates are noisy: the 2022 standard error is 9.5 points.

One older survey snapshot fits the same picture. In the HRS for 2008 and 2010, among people 50 or older who had retired from their last job, 17% left the money in the plan, 43% were receiving benefits from it, 22% rolled it to an IRA and 15% withdrew it.

12Do the laws work? Getting people in and keeping money in

The rules in Section 3 decide when money must come out. A longer list of laws tries to get more workers into plans in the first place and to keep their money there longer. This section reviews 67 federal provisions from the Revenue Act of 1978 to SECURE 2.0, the changes to the federal Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) and the state auto-IRA mandates, and asks what the evidence says about each. The short answer is that laws that change defaults have worked, laws that offer incentives or permissions have shown little so far, and Congress has widened access to withdrawals at the same time as it encouraged staying in.

Getting people in

Automatic enrollment works where it is used. In a classic firm study, 86% of new hires joined when enrollment was automatic, against 37% under the old opt-in rule. In the TSP, which has enrolled new federal hires automatically since August 2010, participation among FERS employees rose from 84.7% in 2009 to 96.2% in 2025, and among those with under two years of service from 70.0% in 2009 to 97.9% in 2012. When the default contribution rose from 3% to 5% in October 2020, the share of contributors putting in less than the full 5% match fell from 30% in 2019 to 11% in 2025. The gain in lifetime saving is smaller than early studies implied. Following nine plans over many years, one recent study puts it at about 0.6% of income a year from automatic enrollment and 0.3% from automatic escalation, three-quarters below the simple estimate, mainly because 42% of balances are cashed out at job changes, employer matches go unvested and fewer workers accept each scheduled step-up.

Automatic enrollment has spread, but the national participation rate has not moved. 401(k) plans reporting it went from 3.9% of plans in 2010 to 31.4% in 2024, and their share of participants with balances from 26% to 54%. Over the same years the share of private-industry workers participating in any plan stayed at 48–53%. Access rose by five points after 2019, mostly at small establishments, but the share of workers with access who join fell from 77% to 72%: new access is reaching workers who are less likely to join, such as part-time, low-wage and small-firm employees. Tax records point to more saving than the employer survey does. The share of people with wages who made an elective deferral to a workplace plan rose from 34.7% in 2008 to 42.8% in 2020, and among those under 26 from 12.1% to 20.8%, where automatic enrollment has the most effect (computed). The two differ partly because W-2s count anyone who deferred at any job during the year, while the survey is a March snapshot, and partly because wage growth moved workers into higher wage bands; within wage bands the rise is 1–4 points. The SECURE 2.0 mandate, which requires automatic enrollment in most 401(k) and 403(b) plans set up after December 2022 from the 2025 plan year, is being followed: 88% of new 401(k) plans filing for 2025 report automatic enrollment, against 30% of plans started in 2019 (computed; 2025 filings are incomplete).

State auto-IRA programs, now open in 15 states, held 1.41 million funded accounts and $3.39 billion at the end of August 2026. Their largest effect may be on employers: in IRS data, about 17% of firms covered by a mandate started their own retirement plan instead, with no sign of firms dropping plans. The federal startup credit, enlarged in 2019 and 2022, was claimed by 5.5% of eligible firms in 2023, up from about 1%.

Keeping money in

Two of the clearest successes are already in this report. Raising the RMD age delayed withdrawals, as the IRS, SCF, CPS and SIPP all show (Figures 4, 15 and 16). The TSP’s 2019 withdrawal options were followed by a 7–10 point rise in the share of leavers who keep a balance (Figure 23). The 2005 rule that moves force-outs of $1,000 to $5,000 into IRAs also keeps money tax-deferred: about 48% of IRA rollovers just above $1,000, and 29% just below $5,000, happen only because of the default. But those IRAs are passive, often in cash-like funds, and far more often abandoned than voluntary rollovers. SECURE 2.0’s auto-portability, which moves small balances to a new employer’s plan, had completed about 42,000 transfers by July 2026 by its operators’ count, a pace of roughly 10% of the annual volume the Department of Labor projected (computed). The final rule has been under White House review since 14 September 2026.

Withdrawals before retirement age are not growing relative to the system. The share of returns paying the 10% additional tax rose from 2.85% in 1996 to 4.18% in 2009 and drifted down to 3.30% by 2019, and the tax per $100 of taxable IRA and pension distributions fell from 77–81 cents in 1996–2010 to 47 cents in 2023. In 2020 the CARES Act exempted coronavirus-related distributions: returns paying the tax fell by 26% and the tax by 40%, (computed), while IRS panel data show total withdrawals before 59½ rising by about 25%. Loosening the penalty moved money out. About nine new penalty-free routes have been added since 2018, among them $1,000 emergency withdrawals from 2024; tax year 2024, the first year for several of them, has not been published. Inside the TSP, 4.9% of FERS participants took a hardship withdrawal in 2025 and 9.5% took a loan, the highest since at least 2015, against 4.0% and 7.0% in 2021.

13Where retirees’ income comes from, and how they spend it

Account withdrawals are one income source among several. This section places them among the others and looks at how older households spend. Household surveys capture only about half of retirement-plan income (Section 9), so the levels below understate pensions and withdrawals and overstate the share from Social Security. Trends within one consistent series are more reliable than levels.

Earnings roughly doubled as a share of income, from 16.8% in 1997 to 30.2% in 2012, and have been 29–31% since, tracking the labor force participation of people 65 or older (11.9% in 1998, 20.2% at the 2019 peak, 19.1% in 2025). Defined benefit pensions fell from about 19% to 13.7%. Account withdrawals were under 1% under the old questions, which missed most of them, and rose from 5.2% to 8.1% under the current ones. The SCF, which asks about withdrawals directly, shows the same shift: withdrawals rose from 5.0% of the income of households 65 or older in 2003 to 8.0% in 2021, and the share of those households drawing on an account from 21.3% to 28.3% (at 75 or older, from 20.2% to 37.2%). Retirement accounts went from 3.5% of the net worth of households 65 or older in 1989 to 15.2% in 2022, and households with only DC-type savings rose from 6.6% to 22.7% while those with only a DB pension fell from 38.6% to 21.6%.

Social Security remains the largest single source for the typical retiree: about half of income for the median household 65 or older in the SCF (49.1% in 2022), and about 35% of aggregate income in the CPS. Receipt at 65 to 69 fell from 83.8% to 64.4% between the 1998 and 2026 surveys as claiming moved later. Linked tax records change the picture. In the CPS for 2012, 64% of aged beneficiary units got half or more of their income from Social Security and 36% got 90% or more; with IRS and SSA records the figures were 50% and 18%. For 2015, with administrative data, pensions and DC withdrawals together (33.2% of income for people 65 or older) overtake Social Security (31.4%) as the largest source; the survey alone gives 23.6% and 35.0%.

Spending

From 1984 to 2024, real spending per household rose 37% at 65 to 74 and 66% at 75 or older, against 20% at 55 to 64. Households 75 or older went from spending 47.5% as much as 55-to-64-year-olds to 65.7%. Most of the remaining gap is household size: per person, those 65 to 74 and 75 or older spend 89–90% of what people 55 to 64 spend. Studies that follow the same households find real spending falling by about 1–3% a year after retirement: 1.9% a year for singles and 2.8% for couples in one study, about 0.8% a year in another, with smaller declines for the wealthy. Housing has grown from 32.1% of the budget of households 65 or older in 1988 to 36.1% in 2024. Health care has stayed at 12–14%, but insurance premiums rose from 42% of it to about two-thirds.

Older households spend nearly all of their income. In 2024, spending by households 65 or older equaled 91% of their pre-tax income, and about 100% at 75 or older, against 70% at 55 to 64. Together with the low withdrawal rates in Sections 5 and 10, this suggests that most retirees spend what they take out while leaving most of their account balances untouched (inferred; no source here follows withdrawals into spending).

14How long the money has to last

How long savings must last sets how fast they can be drawn down. The RMD divisors in Table 3 come from IRS life tables, which were updated for longer lives in 2022. At 73 the divisor is 26.5 years, longer than any figure below, because the table assumes a beneficiary ten years younger than the owner.

Life expectancy at 65 rose from 16.4 years in 1980 to 19.1 in 2010 and 19.6 in 2019, but the gain slowed from about 1.5 years a decade in 2000–2010 to 0.4 in 2010–2019. COVID took 1.1–1.2 years off it in 2020 and 2021 (18.5 and 18.4). By 2024 it was 19.7, a record but only 0.07 years above 2019 and about 0.1 below where the 2010–2019 trend would have put it. The fall was much larger for Hispanic adults (2.8 years), American Indian and Alaska Native adults (2.2) and non-Hispanic Black adults (2.0) than for non-Hispanic White adults (1.1). Every group has recovered, but the non-Hispanic White figure is flat at 19.5 years, so the 2024 record comes from other groups and the changing population mix (inferred).

Early data for 2025 point to a further improvement. NCHS’s provisional age-adjusted death rate for 2025 is 4.6% below 2024, a record low, with falls of 1.7% at 65 to 74, 4.1% at 75 to 84 and 7.6% at 85 or older. Part of the drop may come from new population estimates rather than fewer deaths, and the Trustees’ partial-year estimate shows life expectancy at 65 flat in 2025. The final 2025 life tables will settle it.

For retirement planning the cohort measure matters more, and it has barely moved. Someone turning 65 in 2025 can expect 19.1 more years if a man and 21.7 if a woman, to about 84 and 87, roughly a year more than the period figures. The pre-COVID projection for the same group was 0.1–0.2 years longer. The Trustees project cohort life expectancy at 65 of 19.5 and 22.0 years in 2030 and 20.7 and 23.1 in 2050. On 2024 death rates, 30.7% of 65-year-olds reach 90 and 12.0% reach 95, back from 25.4% and 9.1% in 2021.

Who lives longer

Averages hide wide gaps by income, and they were widening before COVID. Among 40-year-olds in 2001–2014, the richest 1% of men could expect to live 14.6 years longer than the poorest 1%, and the richest women 10.1 years longer than the poorest. Men in the top income quarter gained 0.20 years of life expectancy a year over that period, against 0.08 in the bottom quarter. Across birth cohorts, the National Academies project that the gap in life expectancy at 50 between men in the top and bottom fifths of lifetime earnings widens from 5.1 years for those born in 1930 to 12.7 years for those born in 1960. By education, the gap in life expectancy at 25 between adults with and without a bachelor’s degree grew from 2.6 years in 1992 to 6.3 in 2019 and 8.5 in 2021. Higher earners hold most retirement-account balances, so the average horizon understates how long the typical dollar in these accounts has to last (inferred).

15What to watch

  • The RMD age rises to 75 in 2033. Each earlier increase moved the jump in incidence with it. The same should happen again, leaving people aged 73 and 74 near the roughly 30% incidence now seen at 70 to 72.
  • Roth balances. Roth IRAs held $1.7 trillion of $14.6 trillion in IRA assets in 2023. They received more contribution dollars than traditional IRAs, and $37 billion of conversions from 1.6 million taxpayers. With no lifetime RMDs on Roth IRAs or, since 2024, Roth plan accounts, a growing share of retiree balances will sit outside the rule that now drives withdrawals.
  • In-plan retention. A decade of gains has paused in the newest Vanguard cohorts. Whether retirees begin to use installments and partial withdrawals at scale is the open question for plan design.
  • Rollover volume. Industry projections pass $1 trillion a year before 2030. The age mix suggests most of the growth will come from retirement-age balances.
  • Automatic enrollment for new plans. The SECURE 2.0 mandate took effect for 2025 plan years; its final regulation has been under White House review since June 2026. Complete 2025 Form 5500 filings, and the Saver’s Match from 2027, will show whether new plans enroll and keep workers who did not join before.
  • Auto-portability. The Department of Labor’s final rule has been under White House review since 14 September 2026. Volume so far runs at about 10% of the projection.
  • New data. The 2025 SCF is due in late 2026. It asks about 2024 withdrawals, the second year under RMD age 73, and is the next survey check on the move of the cliff. IRS tables for tax year 2024 will give the second year under RMD age 73. EBRI has published no annual update of its IRA Database since 2017 data, which leaves ICI with the only full annual recordkeeper series on IRA withdrawals after the SECURE Act.

16Data notes

  • IRS withdrawer counts. The number of taxpayers with IRA withdrawals in the IRS study steps up from 13.4 million in 2011 to 17.0 million in 2012, with similar one-year bumps in 2004 and 2008. ICI's survey and database and Form 1040 return counts show no such jump, and the tables give no explanation. Incidence is therefore charted from 2012. Earlier years appear only in Figures 6 and 12, with a caveat.
  • What IRS "withdrawals" include. The tables define withdrawals as distributions reported on Form 1099-R other than trustee-to-trustee transfers, and say that Roth conversions are shown separately. The evidence on whether conversions are inside the totals is mixed. Reported withdrawals rose by $84 billion in 2010, when $65 billion was converted, and fell back in 2011, which suggests they were that year. For 2019 to 2023 the totals sit close to gross IRA distributions on Form 1040, a line that includes conversions: 0.7–2.2% below it in 2019 to 2021, and 3.1% and 1.9% above it in 2022 and 2023. Adding the separately reported conversions would overshoot the Form 1040 line by 4–10%, which argues against conversions being inside the totals in those years (computed). This report treats the series as gross distributions and flags 2010.
  • IRS method change in 2022. In June 2026 the IRS re-released tax year 2022 and released 2023 under a revised method, which adds Forms W-2 to the matched sample, and calls 2022 onward a new series. Earlier years were not revised. Totals and age-level figures for traditional IRAs move little across the break (incidence at 70 to 74 was 69.0 in 2021 and 69.1 in 2022), but the small plan types move a lot: Roth IRA withdrawals went from $5.7 billion in 2021 to $23.8 billion in 2022, and SEP withdrawals from $26.4 billion to $6.7 billion. The 2012–2023 series is therefore consistent only within 2012–2021 and 2022–2023. Tax year 2024 had not been released as of 4 October 2026.
  • Incidence in IRS data is withdrawers divided by taxpayers with a year-end balance. It can exceed 100 at advanced ages. Dollar-weighted rates divide a year's withdrawals by the same age band's balance at the end of the prior year, so the two groups differ by one year of aging.
  • ICI survey. Figures are labelled by the tax year of the withdrawal, which is the survey year minus one. The survey moved from telephone to an online panel in 2016. Its age bands move with the RMD age.
  • ICI database. The provider sample changed in 2017, when the number of investors fell from 12.0 million to 6.2 million. Detailed age tables exist only for report years.
  • EBRI database. Figures for 2011 and 2012 are per account and those for 2013 to 2017 per individual. EBRI issue briefs are available to members only, so some figures come from EBRI's public summaries.
  • Staying in the plan (Section 11). The TSP measure is FRTIB’s strategic metric: FERS participants with a balance above zero one year after separation. FRTIB recalculated fiscal 2015–2016 in November 2016, and the corrected values are used; fiscal 2022 Q3 to 2023 Q3 and fiscal 2025 Q1–Q3 were not found. TSP separated-account counts for 2011–2015 are year-end snapshots and from 2016 the “historical plan participation” chart; a few were read from images. Form 5500 separated participants are “other retired or separated participants with vested right to benefits” in private DC plans. The active count was widened in 2005, and in 2009–2013 small-plan (5500-SF) filers reported every participant as active, so separated participants are understated then. SCF former-employer plans are the Federal Reserve’s FUTPEN plus CURRPEN; CURRPEN starts in 2001, and standard errors use the replicate weights from 2004.
  • Plan laws (Section 12). The provision list (67 federal provisions, 1978–2026) and the evidence behind each verdict are in the project’s research notes. Form 5500 automatic-enrollment shares use pension feature code 2S (automatic enrollment) among plans with code 2J (401(k)), after removing duplicate filings; 2025 filings were incomplete when tabulated. BLS widened its definition of access to retirement benefits in March 2009, so earlier years are not shown, and it most likely does not count state auto-IRAs, because its definitions say a payroll-deduction IRA is not an employer-sponsored plan, so gains in mandate states are missed. W-2 deferrals cover private and public employers and filed returns only, and tax years after 2020 are not yet published. The penalty count understates early withdrawals: the Treasury Inspector General found that for tax year 2021 about half of taxpayers with early distributions neither paid the tax nor filed Form 5329. The penalty measure counts returns with the additional tax on IRAs and other tax-favored accounts (Form 1040 Schedule 2), which also covers some other tax-favored accounts such as health savings accounts; 2018 uses the combined IRA and pension taxable line as its denominator. Recordkeeper and industry figures appear only where no public source exists and are labelled.
  • Income and spending (Section 13). CPS income shares are for people 65 or older, weighted, from Census public-use files. The series has three versions, each break measured within one year: traditional questions (to income year 2013), redesigned questions with the old processing (2013 split sample to 2017) and the updated processing (2016 research file, 2017 bridge file, 2018 on); only the first and last are charted. The updated processing counts interest credited inside retirement accounts as asset income (6.8% of 2025 income); Figure 28 and the shares quoted leave it out. SCF income is for the year before the survey, households with a head 65 or older, and account withdrawals are separate only from the 2004 survey. Consumer Expenditure figures are per consumer unit and deflated with the CPI-U to 2024 dollars; income is imputed from 2004.
  • Longevity (Section 14). NCHS figures are for the US resident population; 1980–2017 are published to one decimal and 2018–2024 to two. The Trustees’ figures are for the Social Security area population and are intermediate assumptions; their 2025 period estimate rests on partial-year data. Race and Hispanic-origin groups start in 2019, The large gain for American Indian and Alaska Native adults is not a change in NCHS’s adjustment for misclassified race; part of it reflects the switch from 2010-based to 2020-based population estimates, though on consistent 2020-based estimates it still rose 1.4 years from 2022 to 2024. Income gaps are race-adjusted expected ages at death for 40-year-olds; no source reachable here covers income gaps after 2014.
  • Vanguard cohorts. Each cohort is observed for a different length of time, so Figure 8 compares cohorts at the same number of years after leaving. How America Retires measures from the end of the retirement year and defines its categories slightly differently from the cohort papers.
  • Rollovers in 2022 (resolved). ICI’s June 2026 report gave $670 billion rolled into traditional IRAs, against $636 billion in the IRS tables. Both are the same IRS cell, from two releases. ICI used the IRS’s first tax-year 2022 release (February 2025). The IRS re-released 2022 under its revised method on 4 June 2026, a day after ICI’s report, and the cell became $636 billion. ICI’s September 2026 data now show $636 billion. The match between $670 billion and the $664 billion all-IRA total was a coincidence. This report uses $636 billion for traditional IRAs and $664 billion for all types. The February 2025 file itself could not be retrieved, so the link rests on ICI’s citation, the dates and the matching cells for other years.
  • Microdata tabulations (Section 10). SCF: Federal Reserve public files, 2004–2022 waves. A withdrawal is the Federal Reserve’s PENACCTWD variable above zero (IRA and Keogh withdrawals plus withdrawals from account-type employer plans); the IRA-only measure uses the three IRA withdrawal items. Estimates pool the five implicates, and standard errors use the 999 replicate weights plus imputation variance. Ownership and median balances for 2019 and 2022 reproduce the Federal Reserve’s published figures. CPS ASEC: Census public-use files, weighted, people 50 or older. Account withdrawals are regular payments from an IRA, Keogh or 401(k) in the files processed the old way and the distribution items (401(k), 403(b), IRAs and others) in files processed the new way. Counts of recipients 65 or older match Census’s published PINC-09 tables to within 0.1% in 2016 and 2021. The CPS series has two breaks, each measured within a single year: the 2014 redesign raised receipt at 65 or older from 1.6% to 9.9% on a split sample, and the 2019 processing change raised it from 8.9% to 15.7% on the same 2018 sample. Only the processing used since 2019, extended back to 2016 and 2017 by Census’s research and bridge files, is charted. SCF withdrawal rates divide the prior year’s withdrawals by the balance at the interview, which is after the withdrawal, so they run slightly above rates on the prior year-end balance; households that emptied an account are excluded. CPS standard errors use Census’s replicate weights: about 0.3 points at 65 or older and 0.4–0.8 points by age group. Census’s weights for pandemic nonresponse lower 2019–2021 shares at 65 or older by 0.2–0.8 points and leave the 2020 dip intact. The jump in the 65-or-older population between ASEC 2025 and 2026 (61.5 million to 65.2 million) is partly new population controls based on the 2020 Census (about 1.65 million) and partly aging; shares are unaffected, but counts across 2025–2026 are not comparable. SIPP: Census public-use files for 2021–2025 (withdrawal years 2020–2024), person level, year-end account holders, 240 replicate weights. The 2021 file asked the withdrawal items only of retired owners, so 2020 is not comparable, and the 2025 file’s topcode inflates dollar-weighted rates for 2024; those years are left out of the rates quoted here. SIPP ownership matches Census’s published wealth tables to within 0.3 points. The long SIPP series (Figures 17 and 18) adds the 1996, 2001, 2004 and 2008 panels: the monthly person-level distribution recode summed over the three core waves before each assets module, people present in all 12 months (91–97% of holders), the module weight and 108 or 120 replicate weights. Census’s current 1996 core files are re-edited longitudinal files whose layout differs from the wave dictionaries Census posts, so their positions come from NBER’s dictionary and were checked against the data. Holders have a combined balance above zero at the end of the window. The 2014–2019 bridge uses ERET_LUMPSUM (2014 panel waves 2–4 and the 2018–2020 files) among the same holders, with 240 replicate weights.
