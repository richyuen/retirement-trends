# How retirement-age participants in US employer DC plans (401(k), 403(b), 457, TSP) take money out, and how that has trended (recordkeeper and industry data)

Research date: 3 October 2026. United States only. "Data year" is stated for every figure; publication year is given separately where it differs.

Reading notes for the report writer:
- Base populations differ by source and are stated each time: share of **participants** vs share of **assets**; **age 60+ terminated** ("retirement-age") vs **all-age terminated** vs **all active participants** vs **plans**.
- Method limits: source PDFs were read through a fetch tool that (a) cuts long PDFs at roughly page 45-50 and (b) returns a machine summary. Tables that feed charts were read twice where possible; that is flagged as "(two matching extractions)". Items read once are flagged "(single extraction)". Chart-only values that were not printed as numbers were not used. Anything I calculated is marked **computed** with inputs.

## 1. Vanguard: what retirement-age (60+) terminated participants do, in the year of termination and about five years later; plan rules on installments and partial withdrawals; all-age preservation

### Takeaway
Across every Vanguard cohort study (cohorts 2004-2020), about 7 in 10 age-60+ leavers and about 9 in 10 of their dollars stay tax-deferred, and the full cash-out share of participants has stayed flat at roughly 21%-29% (about 3%-6% of assets); what changed is *where* preserved money sits: five years out, assets still in the employer plan rose from 18% (2007 cohort, measured 2012) to 32% (2016 cohort, measured 2021) while assets rolled to IRAs fell from 72% to 56%. Plan design moved in step: Vanguard plans allowing ad hoc partial withdrawals rose from 10% (2012) to 43% (2024 and 2025) and plans allowing installments from 58% (2014) to 68% (2024 and 2025); about half of retirees are still in the plan at the end of their retirement year and about a quarter after three years.

### Inferences
- **Computed** in-plan totals (remain without installments + remain with installments), from the two Figure 3 tables above. One calendar year after the termination year: participants 29% (2011 cohort at YE2012: 26+3) vs 32% (2020 cohort at YE2021: 29+3); assets 35% (31+4) vs 46% (41+5). Five calendar years after: participants 13% (2007 cohort at YE2012: 9+4) vs 17% (2016 cohort at YE2021: 13+4); assets 18% (12+6) vs 32% (25+7). Rollover five years after: participants 57% vs 52%; assets 72% vs 56%. Cash five years after: participants 26% vs 26%; assets 4% vs 5%.
- The shift over the decade is from IRA rollover to staying in plan, concentrated in larger balances (asset shares moved much more than participant shares). Cash-out behavior of small-balance retirees did not change.
- The share of participants taking in-plan installments is small and flat (3%-6% of each cohort in the 2013 paper, 3%-4% in the 2023 paper). Rising in-plan retention is mostly money left untouched, not systematic income.
- HAR's "remain" shares at the end of the retirement year (49%-54%) are not comparable with the research papers' first data point, which is measured one full calendar year later (29%-32% of participants). HAR's 2021 cohort one year later (36% at YE2022) is close to the 2023 paper's 2020 cohort (32% at YE2021).
- **Computed** from HAR Figure 10: 2021 retirees preserving at YE2024 = 26% + 41% = 67% excluding, or 76% including, the 9% "roll over and cash out" group; the report text's "about 3 in 4" matches the 76% reading.
- Plan-design series are consistent with a causal story but Vanguard's comparisons are not randomized: plans with flexible options are larger plans with higher balances (median balance of stayers $120,795 vs $73,415).

## 3. ICI retiree surveys of distribution choices: 1995-2000 retirees (published 2000) and 2002-2007 retirees (published 2008)

### Takeaway
In both ICI surveys about half of retirees with a choice took a lump sum (47% of 1995-2000 retirees, 54% of 2002-2007 retirees), about a quarter deferred, 21%-23% chose an annuity and 10% installments; most lump sums were reinvested, mostly in IRAs. Dollar-weighted for 2002-2007 retirees, 47% of balances were reinvested lump sums, 31% annuitized, 19% left in the plan and only 3% spent.

### Cited Findings
- 2008 report sample: 608 respondents who retired between 2002 and 2007 with DC plan assets (mostly 401(k); also 403(b), TSP, 457 and employer-sponsored IRAs); survey fielded 23 Oct - 9 Dec 2007. — [ICI, "Defined Contribution Plan Distribution Choices at Retirement: A Survey of Employees Retiring Between 2002 and 2007" (2008)](https://www.ici.org/pdf/rpt_08_dcdd.pdf)
- Figure S.1, what happened to DC accounts at retirement, % of all respondents (N=608; two matching extractions): lump sum reinvested all 34%; lump sum spent some and reinvested some 11%; lump sum spent all 7%; deferred distribution of entire balance 16%; annuitized entire balance 18%; installment payments 6%; multiple dispositions 9%. — [ICI 2008, Figure S.1](https://www.ici.org/pdf/rpt_08_dcdd.pdf)
- Text: "Just over half (52 percent) of DC plan participants received all of their distributions as a lump sum... the next most prevalent distribution outcome was annuitization of the entire DC balance (18 percent overall), then deferral of distribution (16 percent), and then some combination of dispositions (9 percent). Only 6 percent of retirees received their payouts through installments." — [ICI 2008, summary](https://www.ici.org/pdf/rpt_08_dcdd.pdf)
- Figure S.2, disposition of accumulated balances, % of total DC account dollars (N=468): reinvested lump sum 47%; annuitized 31%; deferred 19%; cashed out (spent lump sum) 3%. ### Gaps
- No later ICI survey with the same design was found, so this series stops with 2002-2007 retirees.
- The 2000 report's dollar-weighted disposition was not extracted.

Rowe Price, "Retaining Retirees in Plans", Feb 2019](https://www.troweprice.com/retirement-plan-services/en/insights/research-findings/retaining-retirees-in-plans.html)

- Cerulli: share of 401(k) plan sponsors preferring to keep retiree assets in plan rose from 26% (2019) to 54% (as reported April 2023). — [NAPA Net, 13 Apr 2023](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2023/4/401k-plan-sponsors-warm-keeping-retiree-assets-plan/)

### Inferences
- Across three recordkeepers the cash-out rate of 60+ leavers by headcount (about 29% at Aon Hewitt in 2013, 31% at Alight for 2008-2017 terminations, 21%-29% at Vanguard) is high but the dollars are small (4%-14% of assets), because cash-outs are concentrated in balances under $5,000-$10,000.
- The time trend toward staying in plan shows up in three independent series: T. Rowe Price assets retained after one year (45% to 61%, 2012-2018), Alight's falling share of 60+ leavers who act within the termination year (69% for 2008 leavers, 61% for 2017 leavers), and Vanguard's cohort tables.
- Systematic withdrawals remain a minority behavior: 3%-6% per year (Alight), 3%-4% of each cohort (Vanguard), 11% of terminated participants 59½+ (T. Rowe Price, 2023-24). Differences in base explain much of the spread (T. Rowe's base is people still in the plan; Vanguard's and Alight's base is everyone who terminated).
- The Fidelity 20% vs 94% contrast and T. Rowe Price's 76%-83% no-withdrawal share before RMD age point the same way as J.P. Morgan/EBRI: for people who keep balances in tax-deferred accounts, the RMD is the effective start date for withdrawals.

### Gaps
- Fidelity: no published time series was found for the share of retirees keeping savings in the plan one or more years after retirement, and nothing on systematic withdrawals; Q4 2024, Q1-Q4 2025 and Q1/Q2 2026 Fidelity analyses contain no retiree withdrawal statistics.
- Empower and Principal: no recordkeeper statistics on retiree distribution behavior were found.
- J.P. Morgan: the primary "Mystery no more" PDF could not be located; no exact figures on the size of the withdrawal jump at RMD age were obtained. The 2025 Guide to Retirement (first 55 pages readable) has no slide on RMD-only behavior.
- T. Rowe Price's year-by-year retention chart (2012-2018; years 2-5 after separation) and its plan-exit chart print no data labels in text; only the 2012 and 2018 one-year values and the 52% four-year exit figure are available. No Reference Point edition later than Midyear 2024 could be opened.
- Alight/Aon Hewitt Universe Benchmarks editions for 2020-2025 were not accessible beyond trade-press headlines, so there is no annual post-termination series by age.

## 5. Thrift Savings Plan (TSP): withdrawals by type, dollars paid out, and the TSP Modernization Act (new withdrawal options effective 15 September 2019)

### Takeaway
Before the TSP Modernization Act, 45% of participants who separated in 2012 had closed their accounts by the end of 2013 and only 4% started monthly payments and 1% bought an annuity; since fiscal 2022 the FRTIB's own metric shows about 68%-73% of FERS participants still holding a TSP balance one year after separation. Total benefits paid by the TSP rose from $12.8 billion (2012) to $49.1 billion (2024). Annual counts and dollars by withdrawal type are published only as unlabeled charts, so a by-type time series could not be compiled.

### Cited Findings
- Pre-Act behavior, participants who separated in 2012, status by end of 2013: "49% Took no action"; "2% Took only a one-time partial withdrawal"; "8% Received a cashout"; "41% Took a full withdrawal", of which 37% received a lump sum (11% transferred the lump sum to another institution, 26% took it as a cash payment), 4% initiated a monthly payment stream, 1% initiated an annuity purchase. "The cash payments only averaged $18,830 while the transferred amount averaged $126,557, resulting in 75% of assets from lump sums going to other financial institutions." — [FRTIB memorandum on TSP withdrawal options, 7 Jul 2015](https://oversightdemocrats.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-oversight.house.gov/files/documents/FRTIB%20MM-2015Jul-Att7.pdf)
- 45% of participants who separated from service in 2012 had closed their account by the end of 2013 (paraphrase of a sentence fragment in the extraction); "in 2013, these withdrawals caused nearly $10 billion to leave the TSP. Almost 72% of that amount was transferred to another financial institution or an employer plan." — [FRTIB Executive Director Greg Long, testimony to the ERISA Advisory Council, 21 Aug 2014](https://dol.gov/sites/default/files/ebsa/about-ebsa/about-us/erisa-advisory-council/AClong082114.pdf)

## 6. Plan-sponsor surveys: share of plans offering installments, systematic or partial withdrawals, and annuity options

### Takeaway
Every survey that can be compared over time shows more plans offering flexible payouts and few offering annuities: Vanguard plans with installments rose from 58% (2014) to 68% (2025) and with partial withdrawals from 10% (2012) to 43% (2025); PSCA respondents with installment payouts rose from 52% (2008) to 59% (2016) while annuity availability stayed at 21%-22%; among Callan's large-plan respondents 73%-82% offered partial distributions and 63%-79% installments in 2021-2025. BLS data for savings-and-thrift plans go the other way on annuities and installments (installments available to 54% of participants in 2000 but 38% in 2017; annuities 34% and 12%).

### Cited Findings
- PSCA annual survey: 52nd survey (plan year 2008): lump-sum payouts 99+%, installment payouts 52%, in-plan and/or purchased annuities 21%. 60th survey (plan year 2016): lump sum 90%, installment payouts 59%, in-plan and/or purchased annuities 22%, in-plan annuities 9.7%. — [PSCA (Jack Towarnicky) written statement to the ERISA Advisory Council, 19 Jun 2018, p. 5](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/EBSA/about-ebsa/about-us/erisa-advisory-council/2018-lifetime-income-solutions-as-a-qdia-towarnicky-written-statement-06-19.pdf)
- PSCA "question of the week" (not the annual survey), July 2023: 70% of respondents allow monthly installments for retirees. — [PSCA, 20 Jul 2023](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2023/7/monthly-installment-payments-retirees/)
- PSCA 67th annual survey (plan year 2023, 709 plans) press release covers SECURE 2.0 distribution provisions only (for example 29.3% adopted the $1,000 emergency withdrawal); the 68th survey (plan year 2024, 755 plans) has a "Retirement Income Offerings" section available only in the paid report. — [PSCA press release, 18 Dec 2024](https://benefitslink.com/articles/psca-survey-results-20241218.pdf); [PSCA survey page](https://www.psca.org/industry-content/surveys/annual-401k-survey/)
- Callan DC Trends Survey (mostly large and mega plans; small samples):
  - 2019 data (2020 survey, 114 sponsors): 63% have a policy on retaining assets of terminated/retired participants and 75% of those focus on retaining assets; 89% have taken steps to prevent plan leakage, most commonly offering partial distributions. — [Callan 2020 DC Trends Survey (text copy)](https://www.readkong.com/page/2020-defined-contribution-trends-survey-4984442)

## 7. Annuitization: TIAA trend and take-up of annuity options in DC plans

### Takeaway
Annuitization is rare and falling wherever it is measured. At TIAA, where annuities are built into the plan, the share of first-time income takers choosing a life annuity fell from 61% (2000) to 18% (2018), while the share whose first income was an RMD rose from 10% to 52%. In 401(k)-type plans and the TSP, annuity purchases are around 1% or less of separating participants.

### Cited Findings
- "The fraction of first-time income recipients who selected a life-contingent annuitized payout stream declined from 61 to 18%" between 2000 and 2018; "the proportion who had a life annuity as part of their payout strategy fell from 52% in 2008 to 31% in 2018. At the same time, the proportion of all income recipients taking an RMD payment rose from 16 to 29%"; "the RMD is becoming the de facto default distribution option for newly-retired TIAA participants." — [Brown, Poterba and Richardson, "Trends in Retirement and Retirement Income Choices by TIAA Participants: 2000-2018", NBER Working Paper 29946, April 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29946)
- "The RMD option was the initial choice of 10% of those who began distributions in 2000, but of 28% by 2007 and an even higher share after the Great Recession. Since simplification of the RMD sign-up process in 2012, the use of RMDs has continued to grow, reaching 54% in 2017 and 52% in 2018." — [NBER w29946, p. 25](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29946/w29946.pdf)
- "Among retirees who made an initial income selection before age 70, the fraction choosing a single-life annuity declined from 31% to 19% between 2000 and 2018, while the fraction choosing a joint-life annuity dropped from 36% to 19%." "Among those who did not draw any retirement income until they were at least 70 years old, 52% in 2000 and 85% in 2018 used an RMD for their initial income draw. The share of this older group selecting a life annuity for its first income draw fell from 41% to about 6%." — [NBER w29946, p. 4](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29946/w29946.pdf)
- "The proportion of retirees taking first income as a systematic withdrawal (SWAT) peaked at 27% in 2006 and has declined to around 17% in 2018"; non-life guaranteed options "peaked in the mid-2000s at around 30% but [have] declined... to about 13% in 2018." — [NBER w29946, p. 25](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29946/w29946.pdf)
- TIAA system size: 3,416,162 participants and 579,426 drawing income in 2008 (17.0%); 4,267,466 participants and 898,990 drawing income in 2018 (21.1%) (Table 2.1; single extraction). Average retirement age rose by about 1.3 years for women and 2 years for men over 2000-2018. — [NBER w29946](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29946/w29946.pdf)
- TSP: 1% of participants who separated in 2012 initiated an annuity purchase; 1,466 annuity purchases in 2013. — [FRTIB memorandum, 7 Jul 2015](https://oversightdemocrats.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-oversight.house.gov/files/documents/FRTIB%20MM-2015Jul-Att7.pdf); [FRTIB testimony, 21 Aug 2014](https://dol.gov/sites/default/files/ebsa/about-ebsa/about-us/erisa-advisory-council/AClong082114.pdf)
- 401(k) plans: about three-quarters of plans covered by GAO's record-keeper questionnaire did not offer an annuity (2014), and none had adopted QLACs. — [GAO-16-433](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-433)
- PSCA testimony, attributing to GAO: "Less than 1 percent of participants in plans covered by our record keeper questionnaire chose annuities"; one record keeper said that in 10 years of offering a lifetime income option "only six clients have adopted it and only three participants have elected it." — [PSCA written statement, 19 Jun 2018, p. 15](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/EBSA/about-ebsa/about-us/erisa-advisory-council/2018-lifetime-income-solutions-as-a-qdia-towarnicky-written-statement-06-19.pdf)
- Availability: annuities offered by 21% (2008) and 22% (2016) of PSCA respondents, in-plan annuities 9.7% (2016); annuity option available to 12% of savings-and-thrift participants in 2017 (see section 6). — [PSCA written statement, p. 5](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/EBSA/about-ebsa/about-us/erisa-advisory-council/2018-lifetime-income-solutions-as-a-qdia-towarnicky-written-statement-06-19.pdf); [BLS, 4 Jun 2018](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2018/lump-sums-are-most-common-payment-option-for-participants-in-defined-contribution-retirement-plans.htm)
- Survey baseline: 18% of 2002-2007 retirees annuitized their entire DC balance and 31% of dollars were annuitized (household recall survey; see section 3). — [ICI 2008](https://www.ici.org/pdf/rpt_08_dcdd.pdf)
- T. Rowe Price: only 4% of retirement income comes from annuities (current and expected), per its 2018 retiree research. — [T. Rowe Price, June 2019](https://www.troweprice.com/content/dam/retirement-plan-services/pdfs/insights/investment-insights/Retirement%20Income%20-%20It%E2%80%99s%20Time%20to%20Get%20Serious%20About%20Retirement%20Income%20Solutions.pdf)

### Inferences
- TIAA is the best-case setting for annuitization (annuity in the default investment menu, a participant base of academics and non-profit staff) and still saw a fall of 43 percentage points in 18 years, so the low take-up in 401(k) plans is unlikely to be only a supply problem.
- The rise of the RMD as first income draw at TIAA (10% to 52%) mirrors the RMD-only behavior in section 4.

### Gaps
- The NBER paper prints year-by-year values for first-income choices only as a chart (Figure 5.1); a first extraction returned a full 2000-2018 table that could not be confirmed as printed data and is **not** used. Only the values quoted in the paper's text are recorded. One passage (p. 24) was extracted as "a majority of participants (52%)" took a life annuity in 2000, which conflicts with the 61% in the abstract; the abstract figure is used.
- No TIAA data after 2018 and no recordkeeper statistics on take-up of newer in-plan annuity or target-date-with-income products (2022-2026) were found.
- The GAO "less than 1 percent chose annuities" sentence was found only as quoted by PSCA; it was not located in my own read of GAO-16-433.

## 8. GAO reports and SECURE Act / SECURE 2.0 provisions that affect DC distributions

### Takeaway
GAO found in 2016 that most 401(k) plans offered neither a withdrawal option nor an annuity, and in 2014 that forced transfers of small balances erode savings; since then SECURE 2.0 raised the force-out limit to $7,000 (2024), ended lifetime RMDs for Roth accounts in employer plans (2024), and moved the RMD age to 73 (2023) and 75 (2033).

### Cited Findings
- GAO-16-433 (published 9 Aug 2016): record-keeper questionnaire responses "represented more than 40 percent of all 401(k) assets and about a quarter of plans at the end of 2014"; "About two-thirds did not offer a withdrawal option—payments from accounts, sometimes designed to last a lifetime"; "About three-quarters did not offer an annuity." GAO made seven recommendations to DOL, including clarifying the annuity-provider safe harbor, liability relief for a mix of lifetime income options, and encouraging RMD-based default distributions. — [GAO-16-433, "401(k) Plans: DOL Could Take Steps to Improve Retirement Income Options for Plan Participants"](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-433)
- GAO-16-433 cites Vanguard: 87% of plans required a full distribution if a partial withdrawal was wanted, and fewer than 1 in 5 retired participants remained in the plan five years after separation. — [GAO-16-433 full report, p. 17](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-433.pdf)
- GAO-19-179 (published 28 Mar 2019), early withdrawals in 2013 by people ages 25-55: "at least $69 billion (+/- $3.5 billion)" in total, of which IRA withdrawals $39.5 billion and employer-plan withdrawals $29.2 billion; hardship withdrawals were about 0.5% of the age group's plan assets and about 8% of their contributions. GAO recommended reporting loan offsets separately on Form 5500 (still open as of April 2026). — [GAO-19-179, "Retirement Savings: Additional Data and Analysis Could Provide Insight into Early Withdrawals"](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-19-179)
- GAO-15-73 (published 21 Nov 2014): plans may transfer balances under $5,000 to IRAs when a separated participant gives no instruction; "Because fees outpaced returns in most of the IRAs analyzed, these account balances tended to decrease over time"; a plan could force out a $20,000 balance if less than $5,000 came from non-rollover contributions. — [GAO-15-73, "401(k) Plans: Greater Protections Needed for Forced Transfers and Inactive Accounts"](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-15-73)
- SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, section 107: "further increases the required minimum distribution age further to 73 starting on January 1, 2023 – and increases the age further to 75 starting on January 1, 2033." — [Senate Finance Committee, SECURE 2.0 section-by-section summary, 19 Dec 2022](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Secure%202.0_Section%20by%20Section%20Summary%2012-19-22%20FINAL.pdf)
- Section 304: the mandatory-distribution (force-out) limit rises "from $5,000 to $7,000, effective for distributions made after December 31, 2023." — [Senate Finance Committee summary](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Secure%202.0_Section%20by%20Section%20Summary%2012-19-22%20FINAL.pdf)
- Section 325: "eliminates the pre-death distribution requirement for Roth accounts in employer plans, effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2023." — [Senate Finance Committee summary](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Secure%202.0_Section%20by%20Section%20Summary%2012-19-22%20FINAL.pdf)
- Other SECURE 2.0 distribution provisions: section 302 cuts the missed-RMD excise tax from 50% to 25% (10% if corrected timely); section 202 allows up to $200,000 (indexed) for a QLAC; section 201 removes RMD-rule barriers to life annuities; section 204 lets an owner aggregate annuity and account distributions for RMD purposes; section 115 allows one emergency withdrawal a year of up to $1,000 (after 31 Dec 2023); section 120 permits automatic portability services; section 303 creates a DOL lost-and-found database; section 327 lets a surviving spouse elect to be treated as the employee for RMDs. — [Senate Finance Committee summary](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Secure%202.0_Section%20by%20Section%20Summary%2012-19-22%20FINAL.pdf)
- Current IRS guidance: "You generally must start taking withdrawals from your traditional IRA, SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA, and retirement plan accounts when you reach age 73"; "Withdrawals from Roth IRAs and Designated Roth accounts (401(k) or 403(b)) are not required until after the death of the account owner"; workplace plan participants "can delay taking their RMDs until the year they retire, unless they're a 5% owner"; missed RMDs face "an excise tax of 25%, 10% if the RMD is timely corrected within two years." — [IRS, RMD FAQs, reviewed 29 Jan 2026](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs)
- 2020 waiver: "The CARES Act enabled any taxpayer with an RMD due in 2020 from a defined-contribution retirement plan, including a 401(k) or 403(b) plan, or an IRA, to skip those RMDs this year." — [IRS news release IR-2020-127, 23 Jun 2020](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-announces-rollover-relief-for-required-minimum-distributions-from-retirement-accounts-that-were-waived-under-the-cares-act)
- RMD age before SECURE 2.0: 70½, then 72 (trade press describing the J.P. Morgan study: "then 70.5, now 72"); T. Rowe Price footnote: "The RMD age is 73 for participants who reached age 72 after 2022." — [PLANSPONSOR, 8 Sep 2021](https://www.plansponsor.com/not-retirement-drawdown-strategy-can-affect-retirement-security/); [T. Rowe Price, Reference Point 2024 Midyear, p. 19](https://www.troweprice.com/content/dam/retirement-plan-services/pdfs/insights/ReferencePoint_Midyear_2024.pdf)
- Early plan response to the $7,000 limit: 57% of Vanguard plans (2024) and 38% of T. Rowe Price plans (30 Jun 2024) used automatic rollover up to $7,000. — [HAR 2025, Figure 4](https://www.nrmlaonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025_How_America_Retires_Report.pdf); [T. Rowe Price, Reference Point 2024 Midyear, p. 14](https://www.troweprice.com/content/dam/retirement-plan-services/pdfs/insights/ReferencePoint_Midyear_2024.pdf)
- TSP Modernization Act of 2017, new withdrawal options effective 15 Sep 2019: see section 5. — [House Report 115-343](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-115hrpt343/html/CRPT-115hrpt343-pt1.htm)

### Inferences
- Rule changes shift the measured series: the 2020 RMD waiver, the move of the RMD age from 70½ to 72 (2020) and 73 (2023), and the end of Roth-account RMDs in plans (2024) all reduce forced withdrawals in the affected years, so "share taking withdrawals" series around 2020-2024 are not purely behavioral.
- The higher force-out limit works against the stay-in-plan trend for small balances, but in dollars the affected accounts are a small share of retirement-age assets.

### Gaps
- No primary-source text was captured for the SECURE Act of 2019 provisions (RMD age 72, annuity-selection safe harbor, lifetime income disclosure); the age-72 change is cited through secondary wording.
- GAO-19-179's breakdown of the $29.2 billion employer-plan figure (cashouts at job separation, hardship withdrawals, unrepaid loans) was not extracted, and that report covers ages 25-55, not retirees.
- The GAO-16-433 chart values by plan size (installments, systematic withdrawals, annuities) are chart-only and are not used.

## 9. What the latest data (2023, 2024, 2025) show

### Takeaway
The newest data points continue the same pattern: about half of Vanguard retirees are still in the plan at the end of their retirement year (49% of 2024 retirees), about a quarter after three years, full cash-outs by 14%-18% in the first year, flexible payout features now in most large plans (68% installments, 43% partial withdrawals at Vanguard in 2025), and most pre-RMD retirees taking nothing.

### Cited Findings
- 2024 retirees (age 60+ terminations), end of retirement year: 49% remain in plan, 29% roll over, 6% roll over and cash out, 16% cash out; 2023 retirees: 51%, 26%, 5%, 18%. 2021 retirees at year-end 2024: 26%, 41%, 9%, 24%. — [HAR 2025, Figures 6 and 10](https://www.nrmlaonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025_How_America_Retires_Report.pdf)
- Data year 2025: 68% of Vanguard plans allowed installments other than RMDs and 43% ad hoc partial distributions (both unchanged from 2024); 83% of terminated participants with access preserved assets and 97% of assets available for distribution were preserved, 3% taken in cash; 48% of plans let terminated participants continue loan repayments; 7% offered automatic portability. — [HAS 2026, pp. 8 and 14](https://workplace.vanguard.com/content/dam/inst/iig-transformation/has/2026/pdf/HowAmericaSaves2026.pdf)
- All-age hardship context, Vanguard: participants using hardship withdrawals (when offered) 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, 6% in 2021-2025. — [HAS 2026, p. 8](https://workplace.vanguard.com/content/dam/inst/iig-transformation/has/2026/pdf/HowAmericaSaves2026.pdf); [NAPA Net, 16 Jun 2026](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/6/plan-design-drives-record-retirement-participation-better-outcomes-vanguard/)
- 2023-2024, T. Rowe Price: 82% (2023) and 83% (2024 through June) of terminated participants age 59½-72 took no withdrawal in the past 12 months; 11% of terminated participants 59½+ were receiving installments (July 2023 - June 2024). — [T. Rowe Price, Reference Point 2024 Midyear](https://www.troweprice.com/content/dam/retirement-plan-services/pdfs/insights/ReferencePoint_Midyear_2024.pdf)
- 2023, Fidelity: 20% of retirees age 70-72 and 94% of retirees age 73+ made 401(k) withdrawals. — [Fidelity, Q4 2023 Retirement Analysis](https://about.fidelity.com/data-and-insights/q4-2023-retirement-analysis)
- TSP: benefits paid $39.5 billion (2023) and $49.1 billion (2024); one-year post-separation retention of FERS participants 69.14% (FY26 Q1) and 69.09% (FY26 Q2); in 2025, 52% of full-withdrawal survey respondents rolled to a traditional IRA and 41% took cash (multiple answers). — [TSF financial statements Dec 2024](https://www.frtib.gov/pdf/reading-room/FinStmts/TSP-FS-Dec2024.pdf.pdf); [FRTIB FY26 Q2 Metrics Report](https://www.frtib.gov/pdf/minutes/2026/May/att4-FRTIB%20FY26%20Q2%20Metrics%20Report%20May%202026.pdf); [FRTIB exit survey, Jan 2026](https://www.frtib.gov/pdf/minutes/2026/Jan/Att9-FRTIB-Full-Withdrawal-Exit-Survey-Results-January-2026.pdf)
- Callan, 2025 data: installment payments 79% and partial distributions 73% of 80 mega-plan sponsors; few offer guaranteed income. — [Callan, 14 Apr 2026](https://www.callan.com/blog/2026-dc-survey/)
- Vanguard, December 2025: "Offering distribution flexibility is necessary but not sufficient for driving utilization." — [Vanguard, "Retirement Income Strategies: Best Practices for Plan Sponsors", Dec 2025](https://workplace.vanguard.com/content/dam/inst/iig-transformation/insights/pdf/2025/251373.03_RI_best%20practices%20paper_v8.pdf)

### Inferences
- First-year "remain in plan" for Vanguard retirees edged down from 53%-54% (2021-2022 cohorts) to 49% (2024 cohort) while rollovers rose from 25%-27% to 29%. Four cohorts are too few to call a reversal, but the latest data do not show further gains in first-year retention.
- No source yet shows retirees using in-plan installments at scale: usage among those who stay is 13% installments and 18% partial withdrawals three years out (Vanguard 2021 cohort in flexible plans).

### Gaps
- No 2025-cohort retiree distribution data (the HAS 2026 distribution section could not be opened and HAR 2026 had not been located as of this research date).
- No 2023-2025 values from ICI, PSCA, Alight, Fidelity (other than the 2023 RMD statistic), Empower or Principal.

## Gaps and caveats

1. **How America Saves distribution sections not readable.** The fetch tool returns only about the first 47-50 pages of each HAS PDF, and the distribution figures sit near page 100. Missing as a result: the annual HAS table of retirement-age participants' behavior by cohort (each edition), the all-age split of terminated participants between cash-out, rollover, installments and remaining in plan, and the plan-size breakdown of distribution options. The cohort evidence in these notes comes from Vanguard's stand-alone papers (snapshots at year-end 2012 and 2021, plus partial information for 2014 and 2015 snapshots) and from How America Retires 2025 (through year-end 2024).
2. **HAS editions not opened at all:** 2010-2014, 2016-2021, 2023, 2024 (dead links; Wayback Machine blocked). Vanguard all-age preservation values for 2015 and 2016 and before 2010 are missing; plan-design values for 2013, 2017 and installment values for 2012, 2016-2018 are missing.
3. **Machine extraction risk.** All PDF values passed through an automated summarizer. Tables A-D, F, G, L (2008-2016, 2019-2022), M, N, O, P, the Aon Hewitt 2013 and Alight age-60+ rows, and the HAS 2026 highlight rows were read twice with matching results; tables A-D also pass a column-sum check (each column sums to 100). Other values are single reads and are marked. Values that a first read produced from charts without printed labels (TIAA year-by-year Figure 5.1, GAO plan-size chart values, T. Rowe Price balance chart, Vanguard RI paper prevalence chart) were discarded.
4. **HAR 2025 Figures 11, 12 and 14** have ambiguous series assignment in the text layer. Only the values confirmed by body text are certain (27% vs 20% remaining at year-end 2024; 54% remaining at year-end 2021 in flexible plans; "15% to 25% less likely to cash out"). HAR's printed text says installments were at 58% and partial withdrawals at 13% "a decade ago" while its Figure 5 shows 59% and 16% for 2015; these are consistent if "a decade ago" means 2014, as PSCA's summary states. One trade article gives "up from 16% in 2015" for partial withdrawals.
5. **Different definitions across Vanguard sources.** The 2013/2023 papers measure cohorts at a single year-end at least one calendar year after the termination year and split "cash" from "combination"; HAR measures from the end of the retirement year and splits "cash out" from "roll over and cash out". HAR's withdrawal-behavior sample (70,000 people, 2014-2018 separations, 10+ years of tenure) is a different, more selected population than the cohort tables. A demographic detail conflicts between two Vanguard write-ups of that sample (median age of non-withdrawers 63 in HAR Figure 16 as extracted, 65 in one extraction of the June 2025 article); it is not used.
6. **ICI recordkeeper survey:** last edition found is First Quarter 2023; no 2023-2025 full-year values; 2018 comes from trade press; long rows in the 2019-2021 editions were garbled by the tool and not used. The series is all-age and for active-plan participants.
7. **ICI 2000 and 2008 reports** are household recall surveys of about 600-660 retirees and include plans other than 401(k)s; they are not comparable with recordkeeper administrative data.
8. **Fidelity, Empower, Principal:** no retiree stay-in-plan series or systematic-withdrawal statistics found; Fidelity's only retiree-specific statistic is the 2023 RMD-age contrast. The Fidelity 2018 split is all-age and reported second-hand.
9. **T. Rowe Price:** intermediate years of the assets-retained chart (2013-2017) and the plan-exit curve are chart-only. The base of the "52% exit within four years" statement is ambiguous (the footnote refers to participants who exited between 2019 and mid-2024). The data set's size is not stated in the pages read. No edition after Midyear 2024 was opened.
10. **Alight/Aon Hewitt:** three different measurement designs (single termination year at year-end; ten termination years at year-end 2017). No annual series by age for 2019-2025.
11. **TSP:** no annual counts or dollars by withdrawal type after 2013 (charts without printed values); no FRTIB quantification of the Modernization Act's effect on partial withdrawals or transfers out; retention metric available for seven quarters only and for FERS participants only; "benefits paid" totals cover all outflow types and all ages; 2025 financial statements not found; the quarter labels of the retention metric rest on matching overlapping values between reports.
12. **PSCA, Callan, BLS:** PSCA retiree-option tables are paywalled (only plan years 2008 and 2016 found). Callan samples are small, skew to very large plans and change yearly; 2020 and 2022 data years missing. BLS found for 2000 and 2017 only, and the two vintages may not be strictly comparable.
13. **TIAA:** covers 2000-2018 only; year-by-year values are chart-only; one text passage as extracted (52% life annuity in 2000) conflicts with the abstract (61%).
14. **J.P. Morgan/EBRI:** figures come from an EBRI release and trade press; the primary J.P. Morgan paper was not opened and no numbers on the size of the withdrawal jump at RMD age were obtained from it.
15. **Legislation:** SECURE 2.0 provisions are quoted from the Senate Finance Committee summary (which mislabels section 304 as "Section 307" in one sentence as extracted); SECURE Act of 2019 provisions were not sourced from a primary document.
16. **403(b) and 457 plans:** apart from TIAA (mostly 403(b)) and the plan mix in ICI's surveys, no separate retiree distribution data for 403(b) or 457 plans were found.
17. **Search limit.** The session's web-search allowance was exhausted partway through, so later work relied on direct fetches of known URLs; several of the gaps above (PSCA, Callan, BLS back editions, Fidelity, Empower, Principal, FRTIB attachments) were not searched as fully as intended.
18. **Coverage period.** No recordkeeper data before 2004 terminations were found; the 2000-2007 period is covered only by the two ICI retiree surveys, the BLS 2000 provisions data and TIAA.
