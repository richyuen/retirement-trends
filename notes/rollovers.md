# Rollovers of retirement savings in the United States (plan-to-IRA and plan-to-plan), with a focus on retirement-age rollovers and trends over time

Scope notes for the report writer (read first):
- "Rollover" below always means a tax-free movement that keeps money inside the retirement system (plan to IRA, plan to plan, or a 60-day IRA redeposit). Withdrawals and cash-outs are only mentioned where a rollover-focused source reports them side by side.
- Two different IRS-based rollover series exist and must not be mixed: (1) IRS SOI Table 1 "Total" row = rollovers into ALL IRA types (traditional, Roth, SEP, SIMPLE); (2) the ICI series = rollovers into TRADITIONAL IRAs only (ICI's tabulation of the same IRS SOI data). They are identical for 2000-2002, 2004-2006 and 2008, and differ for 2007, 2009, 2010 and 2020.
- How the numbers were obtained: IRS and ICI Excel files could not be opened in this research environment (only PDF/HTML could be read, through an automated page reader). Every table below was therefore taken from PDF publications. Where a table could be checked by arithmetic (rows summing to the printed total) or against a second publication, that is stated. Anything I calculated is marked "computed" with inputs.
- Web search budget ran out before every lead could be chased; remaining holes are listed under "Gaps and caveats".

## 1. IRS Statistics of Income (SOI): total rollovers into IRAs by tax year, number of taxpayers, rollovers by age, rollovers vs contributions, Roth conversions

### Takeaway
SOI's matched Form 5498 data show rollovers into IRAs of $225.6 billion (4.08 million taxpayers) in 2000, $299.7 billion (4.32 million) in 2010 and $618 billion (5.7 million) in 2020; rollovers were 4 to 6 times total IRA contributions in every SOI year checked. Taxpayers aged 55 and over supplied 60% of rollover dollars in 2001 and 72% in 2010 (computed), and those aged 60 and over 41% and 54%.

### Cited Findings
Definition
- SOI defines the series this way: "In most cases, the amounts identified as Rollovers on Form 5498 represent withdrawals from employer-sponsored plans (such as 401(k) plans) into traditional IRA plans. Also included are some transfers from one traditional IRA plan to another--but only if the taxpayer makes the withdrawal on his or her own and deposits the amount into another (or even the same) IRA account within 60 days." — [Sailer and Nutter, "Accumulation and Distribution of Individual Retirement Arrangements, 2000", SOI Bulletin Spring 2004](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/00retire.pdf)
- "Trustee-to-trustee transfers are not generally considered rollover", so direct IRA-to-IRA transfers between custodians are outside the series. — [SOI Bulletin article for 2008, table notes](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/12insprbulretirement.pdf)

### Inferences
- Average rollover per taxpayer (computed, amount / taxpayers): 2000 $55,314 (225,636,922k / 4,079,197); 2001 $48,495; 2002 $51,235; 2004 $59,097; 2007 $72,896 (322,336,641k / 4,421,849); 2010 $69,322; 2020 about $108,000 (618bn / 5.7m, rounded inputs). For 2008 the answer depends on which table is used: $48,508 (Table 1) or $69,139 (Table 4).
- Share of rollover dollars from taxpayers aged 55 and over (computed from the by-age tables): 2001 60.0%; 2002 65.8%; 2004 62.5%; 2008 70.8% (Table 4 basis); 2010 71.9%. Aged 60 and over: 40.6%; 46.1%; 43.3%; 53.4%; 53.5%. Share of rollover TAXPAYERS aged 55 and over: 33.8% (2001), 39.8% (2002), 43.4% (2004), 47.5% (2008), 49.9% (2010). So over the 2000s rollovers became both older and more concentrated in the 55-69 band (51.7% of dollars in 2001, 59.7% in 2010).
- Rollovers relative to total IRA contributions, all IRA types (computed): 6.2x in 2000 (225.6 / 36.5); 4.4x in 2004 (214.9 / 48.7); 4.9x in 2008 (272.1 / 55.6); 5.9x in 2010 (299.7 / 51.2).
- The 2008 article's two text statements each match a different table: the "fraction of 1 percent" rise in taxpayers matches Table 4 (4,448,705 vs 4,421,849 in 2007 = +0.6%, computed), while the "33.5 percent" fall in average value matches Table 1 (48,508 vs 72,896 = -33.5%, computed). The 2008 taxpayer count should be treated as unresolved; the $272.1 billion amount is the figure used by both SOI's later Figure B and ICI.
- From 2010 (299.7) to 2020 (618), all-IRA rollovers roughly doubled in nominal dollars while the number of taxpayers rolling over rose about 32% (4.32m to 5.7m), i.e. most of the growth is larger rollovers, not more people (computed from the cited figures).

Taxpayer counts for 2005, 2006 and 2009 are also only in Excel (05in01ira.xls, 06in01ira.xls, 09in01ira.xls).

- The 2000 by-age table could be read but its rollover and withdrawal columns appear to be offset by one age row relative to 2001-2010 patterns (details under "Gaps and caveats"); it is excluded from the chart-ready series.
- SOI published no IRA study for tax year 2003 and none before the 2000 match other than the 1996-1999 tabulations that ICI reports.

## 2. ICI: traditional-IRA rollovers vs contributions by year; IRA Owners Survey; IRA Investor Profile (rollovers by age, new IRAs opened with rollovers)

### Takeaway
ICI's IRS-based series shows rollovers into traditional IRAs rising from $114.0 billion in 1996 to $516.7 billion in 2018, about $595 billion in 2020 and $670 billion in 2022, and making up 89% to 96.5% of traditional IRA inflows every year. In ICI's household survey about six in ten traditional-IRA households hold rollover money, about 85% rolled over their entire balance, and the share citing retirement as a reason for the rollover rose from 27-33% around 2008-2012 to 43-46% in 2022-2025.

## 3. EBRI IRA Database: rollovers vs contributions, average and median rollover

### Takeaway
In EBRI's administrative IRA database, rollover dollars were 12 to 16 times contribution dollars in each year from 2011 to 2017, and the average rollover into a traditional IRA was about $94,000-$102,000 against a median of about $14,000-$26,000.

### Cited Findings
- 2010: "The average and median rollover amounts were $69,012 and $17,614 respectively, compared with the average contribution of $3,335." — [EBRI Issue Brief summary, 30 May 2012](https://www.ebri.org/content/individual-retirement-account-balances-contributions-and-rollovers-2010-the-ebri-ira-databasetm-5068)
- 2011: "almost 13 times the amount of dollars were added to IRAs through rollovers than from contributions"; "approximately 1.1 million accounts received rollovers in 2011" while more than 1.6 million accounts received contributions; database of 20.5 million accounts, 16.6 million individuals, $1.456 trillion. — [EBRI Issue Brief summary, 23 May 2013](https://www.ebri.org/content/individual-retirement-account-balances-contributions-and-rollovers-2011-the-ebri-ira-database-5214); [PLANADVISER, 23 May 2013](https://www.planadviser.com/rollovers-rule-the-ira-roost/)

### Inferences
- The EBRI ratios (12-16x) are lower than the ICI/SOI traditional-IRA ratios (23-28x in the same years) because EBRI's denominator includes Roth IRA contributions, which are large relative to Roth rollovers.
- The database shrank sharply between the 2016 and 2017 updates (24.2 million to 11.3 million accounts), so the drop in the median rollover from $21,383 to $14,454 may reflect a change in contributing providers rather than behaviour.

### Gaps
- Average and median rollover by age of owner, and the share of rollover dollars from owners aged 60 and over, are in the full EBRI Issue Briefs, which sit behind a member login; only the public summaries were readable. No EBRI by-age rollover figure is reported here.
- Summaries for the 2012 and 2013 data years were not located, and no EBRI IRA Database update after the 2017 data year was found.

- Rollovers into traditional IRAs (ICI/SOI) are of the same order as private DC plan disbursements: the ratio (computed) was 106% in 2000, 105% in 2005, 100% in 2010, 102% in 2015, 94% in 2018, 85% in 2020 and 94% in 2022 (670 / 716.0). ## 7. Total IRA and DC plan assets (context for sizing rollover flows)

### Takeaway
IRAs are now the largest pool of US retirement assets: $19.2 trillion at year-end 2025 (39% of $49.1 trillion) against $14.2 trillion in employer DC plans, and $19.9 trillion versus $15.0 trillion at mid-2026. ICI's asset estimates were revised upward between Fact Book editions, so published year-end figures from different vintages should not be chained.

## 8. Rollover behaviour by age and balance; where rollovers go; Pew on fees; GAO-13-30

### Takeaway
Among Vanguard participants aged 60 and over who left their employer, the share who had rolled to an IRA by end-2021 falls steadily by cohort, from 57% of participants (66% of assets) for 2011 leavers to 38% (45% of assets) for 2020 leavers, while the share still in the plan rises from 7% to 32% of participants; because older cohorts have had more years to act, this is partly timing, but Vanguard reads it as a real shift toward staying in plan. About 26-28% of participants (4-6% of assets) take cash in every cohort.

- Adding the Vanguard categories, the share of participants with money preserved in the plan or an IRA is 64% for the 2011 cohort and 70% for the 2020 cohort, and of assets 82% and 91% (sums of the cited columns; matches the "preserved" figures in the paper). The mix within "preserved" is what changed: IRA rollover is losing share to in-plan retention among retirement-age leavers.
- The cohort comparison overstates the shift because each cohort is observed for a different length of time (ten years for 2011, one year for 2020) and people roll over gradually; GAO's 50%-after-one-year versus 20%-after-five-years figure shows how much retention decays. The like-for-like evidence in the Vanguard paper (its Figure 6, in-plan retention by years since termination) was described but its values were not extracted.
- Rollover dollars are concentrated among people at or near retirement on every dataset: 54% of SOI rollover dollars came from taxpayers aged 60+ in 2010 (computed), about 44% from ages 60-74 in ICI's 2007-2008 database, "almost 80%" from participants over 50 in Cerulli's 2014 data, and 73% from ages 45-70 in ICI's 2023 database.

### Gaps
- Recordkeeper statistics for separating participants aged 60+ from Fidelity and Alight, and Vanguard's How America Saves distribution tables, were not collected here (another researcher covers recordkeeper distribution data; search budget also ran out).
- The Pew survey of near-retirees and recent retirees behind the "46%" figure was not opened directly; the figure is quoted via DOL coverage.
- No study was found that tracks, on a consistent one-year-after-separation basis over a long period, the share of age-60+ leavers who roll over; the Vanguard cohort table is the closest available.

## 9. Reverse flows and plan-to-plan moves: roll-ins, auto-portability, SECURE 2.0

### Takeaway
Plan-to-plan rollovers are small next to IRA rollovers, about 8% of rollover dollars leaving DC plans ($66 billion and 0.7 million accounts in 2022 per Cerulli/DOL). Auto-portability went live in late 2023 through the Portability Services Network; adoption reached about 21,000 plans by September 2025, but completed transfers so far number in the hundreds to thousands.

### Cited Findings
- Plan-to-plan volume: "0.7 million defined contribution plan accounts with $66 billion in assets were rolled over to other employment-based plans" in 2022; plan-to-plan is "about 8%" of rollover assets over 2021-2023. — [Financial Planning, 30 April 2024](https://financial-planning.com/list/key-provisions-of-the-dol-retirement-advice-rule); [WealthManagement.com, 15 July 2024](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/rpa-news/the-future-of-ira-rollovers)
- Portability Services Network (PSN): six recordkeeper members (Alight, Vanguard, Fidelity, Empower, TIAA, Principal) with Retirement Clearinghouse; "in its first year of operation — more than 15,000 plans representing approximately 5 million participants have signed up"; as of 1 December 2024 "549 auto portability transactions have been completed" and 7,841 were in motion; about 40% of signed plans were live. — [PSCA, 4 December 2024](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2024/12/portability-services-network-now-covers-5m-participants-15000-plans/)
- Adoption in 2025: 20,997 plans had adopted auto portability as of 30 September 2025 (up 40% over three quarters); Fidelity reported 9,200 of its 26,500 corporate DC plans; Empower "over 11,000" plans. — [Retirement Clearinghouse, 5 December 2025](https://blog.rch1.com/blog/2025-a-breakthrough-year-for-portability)
- Auto portability "became operational in late 2023"; Vanguard analysis: "By year-end 2025, 7% of plans had adopted auto-portability"; Retirement Clearinghouse reports consolidating "more than 525,000 retirement savings accounts, comprising more than $20 billion in assets" across all its services (16 December 2025). — [Retirement Clearinghouse, recent developments page](https://rch1.com/auto-portability/recent-developments?page=1)
- Plan sponsor survey (PSCA, September 2024): 6% had implemented or planned to implement auto portability; 25.6% were considering it. Projection quoted by the network: "$1.6 trillion in additional savings would be preserved" over 40 years. — [PSCA, 4 December 2024](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2024/12/portability-services-network-now-covers-5m-participants-15000-plans/)
- SECURE 2.0 provisions: Section 120 permits "automatic transfer of a participant's default IRA ... into the participant's new employer's retirement plan" (effective 12 months after enactment); Section 304 "increases the limit from $5,000 to $7,000" for mandatory cash-outs, for distributions after 31 December 2023; Section 303 creates a national online "lost and found" database at the Department of Labor within two years; Section 324 requires Treasury to issue "sample forms for direct rollovers" by 1 January 2025; Section 126 allows 529-to-Roth IRA rollovers of up to $35,000 lifetime after 2023; Section 325 ends pre-death RMDs for Roth accounts in employer plans after 2023. — [Senate Finance Committee, SECURE 2.0 section-by-section summary](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Secure%202.0_Section%20by%20Section%20Summary%2012-19-22%20FINAL.pdf)

### Inferences
- On current evidence roll-ins and auto-portability do not materially offset the plan-to-IRA flow: a few thousand automated transfers and $66 billion a year of voluntary plan-to-plan rollovers compare with $600-800 billion a year going to IRAs. Their relevance is to small-balance job changers, not retirement-age rollovers.
- Section 304's higher $7,000 cash-out threshold enlarges the pool of small balances that can be forced out into default IRAs, which is the population auto-portability is designed to move back into plans.

### Gaps
- No aggregate statistic on roll-ins to employer plans (dollars or counts) other than the Cerulli/DOL 2022 figure was found; Form 5500 lumps rollover contributions into "other contributions".
- The status of the Department of Labor's implementing regulation for the Section 120 exemption, and cumulative dollars moved through PSN to date, were not found.

## Gaps and caveats

Data access
- Excel files (IRS SOI Tables 1 and 4 for 2004-2023; ICI "The US Retirement Market" tables; ICI Investor Profile supplements) could not be opened. Direct downloads were blocked in the research environment and the page reader returns Excel as unreadable binary. The single most valuable follow-up is to open https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/YYin01ira.xlsx and YYin04ira.xlsx for 2011-2023 (YY = two-digit year; .xls for 2016 and earlier) and ICI's https://www.ici.org/statistical-report/ret_26_q2_data.xls (Tables 11 and 12) to fill 2011-2019 and 2021-2023 all-IRA totals, taxpayer counts, by-age rollovers, and the missing 2021 and 2023 traditional-IRA values.
- All numbers were read from PDFs or web pages by an automated reader, which can misalign table columns. Checks applied: SOI by-age tables for 2001, 2002, 2004, 2008 and 2010 sum to their printed totals; the ICI Figure 1.2 series is identical across three report editions and matches the rounded figures ICI quotes in nine annual reports plus Pew and CRS; DOL Abstract values are consistent with statements in the Abstracts themselves (2010 assets +16%; 2015 assets -0.6%; 2020 DC net outflow $117.7 billion). Single-read values that could not be cross-checked: SOI 2005, 2006 and 2009 totals (Figure B of the 2010 article), ICI survey percentages by year, EBRI summaries, Vanguard cohort table, BEA series, LIMRA series.
- The web search allowance was exhausted partway through, so Cerulli figures for 2013-2020 and 2023-2025, recordkeeper (Fidelity, Alight) rollover statistics, the Pew survey itself, and Federal Reserve Financial Accounts totals were not pursued.
- Long Federal Register documents (2024 Retirement Security Rule; PTE 2020-02) and the DOL Historical Tables PDF could only be read up to roughly page 20, short of the regulatory impact analyses and of Table E16. The 2024 RIA figures are therefore from Financial Planning and CNBC summaries.

Definitional cautions
- IRS "rollovers" (Form 5498) include some 60-day IRA-to-IRA redeposits as well as plan-to-IRA rollovers, and include DB lump sums and money from 403(b), 457 and federal plans; ICI describes the same data as transfers "from employer-sponsored (DC or DB or both) retirement plans". Cerulli counts only DC plan money; LIMRA counts "retail" IRA rollovers. Levels are not interchangeable.
- SOI all-IRA totals and ICI traditional-only totals coincide for 2000-2006 and 2008 but differ in 2007 (322.3 vs 316.6), 2009 (264.3 vs 257.3), 2010 (299.7 vs 288.4) and 2020 (618 vs 594.8). For 2020 the difference is Roth rollovers of $17.5 billion plus a residual of about $5.7 billion (computed: 618 - 594.8 - 17.5), presumably SEP and SIMPLE IRAs.
- Form 5500 "benefits disbursed" and BEA "benefit payments and withdrawals" include rollovers together with cash payments; neither isolates rollovers. Ratios in table N are scale comparisons, not shares.
- ICI survey reasons are multiple-response; the IRA Owners Survey changed mode in 2016; ICI's household counts for 2014-2015 (15 million) reflect a 2014 sampling change in the companion household survey.
- ICI IRA Investor Database results change age base (25-74, then 18-74, then 18+) and provider sample between report vintages; do not splice the incidence or median series across vintages without a visible break.

Conflicts and unresolved points
- Tax year 2008 in the SOI Bulletin: Table 1 and Figure A show 5,609,522 taxpayers and $272.1 billion; Table 4 (by age) totals 4,448,705 taxpayers and $307.6 billion. The article text supports each in different sentences. The by-age table for 2008 is internally consistent and is reported on its own basis; use shares rather than levels from it.
- Tax year 2000 by-age table (https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-soi/00retire.pdf, Table 5): as read, the rollover column gives 15 under 20: 35,579 taxpayers / $176,816 thousand; 20 under 25: 256,415 / 1,679,331; 25 under 30: 384,749 / 5,478,747; 30 under 35: 474,721 / 13,385,572; 35 under 40: 488,891 / 18,522,357; 40 under 45: 482,608 / 23,416,358; 45 under 50: 465,470 / 27,925,886; 50 under 55: 485,921 / 44,901,778; 55 under 60: 447,186 / 44,230,589; 60 under 65: 292,595 / 26,744,140; 65 under 70: 168,254 / 14,112,684; 70 under 75: 63,333 / 3,178,212; 75 under 80: 26,005 / 1,194,726; 80 and over: 7,472 / 689,726 (sums match the total of 4,079,197 / $225,636,922 thousand). Compared with 2001-2010, every value appears one age band too young (for example 2001 shows 37,976 taxpayers at 20-24 and 451,456 at 55-59), and the withdrawal column in the same table shows the same offset (the jump expected at age 70 appears at 65-69). Either the published table or the text extraction is misaligned. Do not chart the 2000 age distribution without checking the PDF visually; the 2000 total is unaffected.
- SOI 2004 by-age table shows $21.7 billion at ages 35-39, roughly double neighbouring ages; it is as printed (the column sums exactly) and is probably a sampling outlier.
- Figure B of the SOI 2010 article was read with contributions and Roth conversions partly transposed for 2008-2010; the values given above for 2009 contributions ($50.7 billion) and 2009 Roth conversions ($6.8 billion) rest on aligning the figure with Tables 1 and 4 (2008 Roth conversions $3.7 billion; 2010 contributions $51.2 billion and Roth conversions $64.8 billion) and on ICI's Figure 1.2 (2009 Roth conversions 6.8). Rollover values in Figure B are not affected.
- ICI IRA asset totals were revised upward between the 2024 and 2025 Fact Books (year-end 2023 $13.6 trillion; year-end 2024 $17.0 trillion); no consistent-vintage asset series was obtained.
- The 2022 rollover estimates span $670 billion (ICI/IRS, traditional IRAs) to $779 billion (Cerulli, DC plans to IRAs); the report should name the source whenever it quotes a 2022 figure.
- The ICI figure "$670 billion in 2022" appears as the latest available year in both the March 2025 and June 2026 editions of ICI's IRA report, although IRS tax year 2023 tables are listed on the IRS site; I could not confirm whether ICI has published a 2023 value.
