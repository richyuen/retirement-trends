# US household surveys on retirement account withdrawals by older and retired households (SCF, CPS ASEC, HRS, SIPP)

Reading notes for the writer (apply to every section):
- Research date: 3 October 2026. All documents were read through an automated fetch tool that summarises pages and PDFs; direct file download (Excel/CSV) was not possible. Numbers marked "2x" were returned identically by two independent reads of the source; numbers marked "1x" came from a single read and should be spot-checked before publication. Numbers marked "computed" were calculated by me from the inputs shown.
- "Data year" is the year the behaviour or income refers to, not the publication year.
- Three categories are kept apart wherever the source allows: (a) partial withdrawals (money drawn from an account that stays open), (b) full cashouts (lump sum taken and not rolled over, account emptied), (c) rollovers (money moved to another tax-qualified account, not income).

## 1. SCF (triennial, 1989 to 2022): ownership, balances, what the survey asks, and SCF-based withdrawal evidence

### Takeaway
The SCF shows retirement account ownership among households headed by someone 65 or older roughly doubling, from 22.8% in 1992 to 40.8% in 2007 and 47.0% in 2022, but the Federal Reserve publishes no regular series on the share of older households taking withdrawals. The best SCF withdrawal evidence is indirect: after the Fed moved the IRA withdrawal question from the income section to the IRA section in 2004, the share of IRA-owning households reporting a withdrawal jumped from 0.5% (1998) and 1.7% (2001) to 17.4% (2004) and 19.9% (2007), and SCF aggregates now match tax data closely (99% of administrative retirement plan income in the 2016 survey).

What the SCF asks
- 2022 questionnaire outline, Section N (financial assets): "Have any IRA/Keogh accounts?" then "Made withdrawals last year? How much?" ### Inferences
- The 1998 to 2004 jump in SCF withdrawal reporting (0.5% to 17.4% of IRA households) is a questionnaire artefact, not a behavioural change. Any SCF withdrawal series must start in 2004 (data year 2003).
- Ownership among the 75+ rose much faster than among 65 to 74 (6.2% in 1992, 30.0% in 2007, about 42% in 2022), consistent with IRA and 401(k) cohorts ageing into the oldest group; the pool of households subject to required distributions has therefore grown even with constant behaviour.
- Because 17 to 20% of all IRA households reported a withdrawal in 2003 and 2006, and tax data show that most withdrawals are made by people over 70, the SCF incidence among 65+ IRA households is very likely well above 20%, but no published SCF figure for that group was found.

### Gaps
- No published SCF table of the share of 65+ (or retired) households with withdrawals from IRAs and pension accounts by survey year (PENACCTWD > 0) was found; it can be computed from the public microdata but I could not do that here.
- The Fed's by-age historic tables (Excel) and chartbook CSV could not be read, so there is no single-vintage 1989 to 2022 series for 65 to 74 and 75+. Missing entirely: 1989 by age, 2010 by age, 2016 by age, the 75+ ownership rate for 2019; medians for 2004 and 2007 by age. 2013, 2019 and 2022 by-age values are from secondary sources and rounded.
- No published analysis was found of the SCF "payout or rollover from a pension plan" questions for retirement-age recipients.
- No Federal Reserve FEDS Note or Urban Institute paper using the SCF specifically to measure withdrawals by retirees was located.

## 2. CPS ASEC: underreporting, the 2014 redesign, the 2019 processing change, and linked-record evidence

### Takeaway
Before 2014 the CPS counted retirement account money only when it was received as a regular payment, so it missed almost all IRA and 401(k) withdrawals: for 2012, 37% of people 65+ reported retirement income in the survey against 61% in linked IRS Form 1099-R records, and the false-negative rate was 81% for people whose only distributions were from IRAs. The 2014 redesign and the 2019 processing update raised reported retirement-account income several-fold, but linked-record studies for 2015 still find 47% (survey) against 69% (administrative) receipt, and CRR finds the 2017 CPS captures only 47% of administrative retirement plan dollars.

### Cited Findings

What the traditional questionnaire measured
- Traditional question: "During 2012 did (you/ anyone in this household) receive any pension or retirement income from a previous employer or union, or any other type of retirement income (other than Social Security or VA benefits)?" The traditional CPS ASEC "only sought to count payments from IRAs and other defined contribution accounts to the extent they were received regularly." — [Bee and Mitchell, "Do Older Americans Have More Income Than We Think?", SEHSD Working Paper 2017-39, pp. 7 and 9](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- "The Census Bureau does not measure distributions from retirement accounts in the Current Population Survey (CPS) or the American Community Survey unless they are received as annuities." — [Iams and Purcell, Social Security Bulletin 73(2), 2013](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v73n2/v73n2p77.html)
- "Many IRA withdrawals are taken as lump-sum distribution rather than as a regular, ongoing payment. Such lump-sum payments are excluded from the CPS ASEC income definition, and thus represent a difference in the respective scopes of the two data sources rather than measurement error per se." (2010 CPS ASEC against tax year 2009 Form 1099-R.) — [Bee, "An Evaluation of Retirement Income in the CPS ASEC Using Form 1099-R Microdata," Census, 2013](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2013/demo/Bee-PAA-paper.pdf)

Bee and Mitchell (2017): CPS ASEC linked to IRS and SSA records, income year 2012 (2013 survey), plus 1990, 1995 and 1998 to 2012
- Receipt of retirement income (DB pensions plus DC and IRA distributions), persons 65+, 2012: 37% in the survey, 61% in administrative records. (2x) Trend: survey 40% in 1990 and 36% in 2012; administrative records 45% in 1990 and 61% in 2012. (2x; intermediate years appear only in the paper's Figure 5.) — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, Table 6 and Figure 5](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- False negatives: people 65+ who have retirement income in the records report none in the survey 46% of the time. By type: 40% for those with only employer-plan distributions, 35% for those with both, 81% for those with only IRA distributions. By size of the 1099-R amount: 72% in the bottom quintile, 31% in the top. Even recipients of large government DB plans (federal CSRS, military, CalPERS, CalSTRS) fail to report 14% to 25% of the time. — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, Tables 5 and 6, Appendix Table 8](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- Among the aged in 2012, "50 percent receive employer-sponsored distributions, while only 29 percent receive IRA distributions"; DB income remains "the predominant source of retirement income for most of the aged in 2012." Example, fifth income decile: 82% have retirement income in the records; 65% DB, 36% IRA, 4% other DC; conditional mean DB $11,100, IRA $5,100, other DC $3,600 (2012 dollars). — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, p. 19 and Table 8](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- Dollars: the CPS captures 48% of the administrative retirement income target for persons 65+ (45% for all adults); in the records retirement income is 34% of total income of persons 65+. Dollar aggregates were not returned by the fetch. — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, Table 1](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- Consequences, householders 65+, 2012: median household income $33,800 in the survey and $44,400 with administrative records (30% higher); poverty rate about 9.1% against 6.9%. Unit: household for income, person for poverty; 2012 dollars. — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, Tables 2 and 3](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)
- Definition of the benchmark: "count all gross distributions that permanently leave tax-preferred retirement plans as income"; direct rollovers, Section 1035 exchanges and Roth conversions are excluded; indirect rollovers cannot be excluded but "are only 2.8 percent of aggregate gross distributions for those aged 55 and over" (citing Argento, Bryant and Sabelhaus). — [Bee and Mitchell 2017, pp. 8, 10, 19](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2017/demo/SEHSD-WP2017-39.pdf)

The 2014 redesign (split-panel test in the 2014 ASEC, income year 2013; full use from the 2015 ASEC)
- New wording: the redesigned ASEC "specifically asks if anyone in the household has a pension, and then if anyone has a retirement account (such as a 401(k), 403(b), IRA, or other account designed specifically for retirement savings) ... The instrument proceeds to inquire if there was a withdrawal or distribution from that retirement account"; for respondents over 70 the wording adds "including distributions you may have been required to take"; follow-ups ask whether amounts were rolled over. — [Semega and Welniak, "The Effects of the Changes to the CPS ASEC on Estimates of Income," 2015](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2015/DEMO/ASSA-Income-CPSASEC-Red.pdf)
- Effect, income year 2013, all ages: "a 419.5 percent increase in people that received income from an IRA, Keogh, and/or 401(k) using the redesigned ASEC and a 230 percent increase in aggregate income from retirement accounts" (slides give 230.1%). Median household income, householders 65+: $35,611 traditional, $37,252 redesigned, 4.6% higher (2013 dollars). (2x) — [Semega and Welniak 2015 paper](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2015/DEMO/ASSA-Income-CPSASEC-Red.pdf); [slides, 20 March 2015](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2015/demo/2015-Welniak-01.pdf)
- EBRI on the same test, persons 65+, 2013: total income 9.1% larger (almost $133 billion in aggregate); retirement income 27.9% larger (almost $71 billion); income from IRAs and 401(k)-type plans "more than 250% higher" than under the traditional questions. — [Copeland, EBRI Notes, 28 May 2015](https://www.ebri.org/content/-characteristics-of-the-population-with-consumer-driven-and-high-deductible-health-plans-2005-2014-and-examining-the-new-income-measures-in-the-current-population-survey--3227); numbers as reported in [PLANADVISER, 28 May 2015](https://www.planadviser.com/government-improves-measure-of-retirement-income/)
- Persons 65+ reporting pension/annuity income: 30.6% in 2013 before the redesign, 35.4% in 2014 and in 2016. Mean among recipients $18,628 (2013, traditional), $20,317 (after the redesign), $22,335 (2016); median $12,300, $13,212, $14,358 (nominal). "Even after the redesign, the aggregated CPS pension/annuity income only amounted to approximately 55 percent to 65 percent of the similar income reported by the IRS." (2x) — [Copeland, EBRI Issue Brief, 12 June 2018](https://www.ebri.org/content/current-population-survey-issues-continue-for-retirement-plan-participation-and-retiree-income-estimates)
- SSA: estimates of the prevalence of retirement income other than Social Security were about 50% higher under the redesigned questionnaire; the 2015 CPS (2014 reference year) "asked separate questions about retirement account withdrawals and distributions." — [Dushi, Iams and Trenkamp, Social Security Bulletin 77(2), 2017](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v77n2/v77n2p1.html); [Dushi and Trenkamp, ORES Working Paper 116, 2021](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/workingpapers/wp116.html)

The 2019 processing change (updated processing system; bridge comparisons on the 2017 and 2018 ASEC files)
- The legacy system recoded redesigned-questionnaire answers into the pre-redesign variables. The updated system "includes specific edits for defined-contribution retirement accounts ... For individuals that report rollovers, but not the amount of the rollover, the allocation variable is the share of the withdrawal that is rolled over. Withdrawals net of rollovers are included in money income variables." Top codes for items such as retirement income were raised from $99,999 to $999,999. — [Rothbaum, "Processing Changes to Income in the CPS ASEC," SEHSD Working Paper 2019-18](https://census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2019/demo/sehsd-wp2019-18.pdf)
- Updated versus legacy processing, all persons, income year 2016: IRA/Keogh/401(k) income recipients +73.7%, mean +8.5%, aggregate +88.4%; total retirement income recipients +4.2%, aggregate +6.0%. Income year 2017: IRA/Keogh/401(k) recipients +73.3%, mean +14.8%, aggregate +98.9%; total retirement income recipients +5.3%, aggregate +9.0%. Pension categories fell (for 2016: company or union recipients -4.2%, federal -5.8%, military -18.1%, state and local -11.6%) because duplicate pension entries were removed. (2x for the IRA/Keogh/401(k) rows) — [Rothbaum 2019, Tables 2 and 8](https://census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2019/demo/sehsd-wp2019-18.pdf)
- Median household income, householders 65+: income year 2016 $39,820 legacy, $40,530 updated (+1.8%); income year 2017 $41,130 legacy, $41,300 updated (+0.4%). Nominal dollars. (1x) — [Rothbaum 2019, Tables 3 and 9](https://census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2019/demo/sehsd-wp2019-18.pdf)

Linked-record evidence after the redesign (income year 2015; 2016 CPS ASEC and 2016 HRS)
- Persons 65+ with pension income including IRA and DC distributions: CPS public-use 47.2%; CPS linked to SSA and IRS records 69.0% (gap 21.8 points); HRS public-use 59.2%. Share of aggregate income from pensions: CPS 21.7%, CPS with records 35.9%, HRS 25.0%. Social Security reliance: 50% or more of family income 52.5% (CPS), 39.9% (with records), 49.8% (HRS); 90% or more 25.6%, 13.8%, 21.2%. Poverty 8.8%, 7.1%, 6.6%. "Underreporting of retirement income continues to be an issue with the public-use data." (2x for receipt and reliance) — [Dushi and Trenkamp, "Improving the Measurement of Retirement Income of the Aged Population," ORES Working Paper 116, Tables 2, 4, 5](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/workingpapers/wp116.html)
- Same data, later Census paper: reliance on Social Security for half or more of income 53% (CPS ASEC), 49% (HRS), 42% (administrative data); poverty 65+ 8.8%, 7.4%, 6.4%. Underreporting "remains prevalent in both surveys," with the HRS doing better than the CPS. Only the abstract could be read. — [Bee, Dushi, Mitchell and Trenkamp, CES-WP-24-32, June 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/CES-WP-24-32.html)
- Why the gap persists: the redesign added explicit questions on withdrawals from 401(k), 403(b) and IRA accounts, but "respondents are still primed with questions about the frequency of these withdrawals (i.e. whether payments are received weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annually), likely limiting responses for one-time or irregular distributions." — [Chen, Munnell and Sanzenbacher, CRR Issue in Brief 18-20](https://crr.bc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/IB_18-20.pdf)

### Inferences
- CPS-based statements that "retirement income receipt among the aged is flat" (40% in 1990, 36% in 2012) are a measurement artefact; administrative receipt rose 16 points over the same period, mostly because IRA and DC distributions grew.
- The CPS has three incompatible regimes for retirement account income: the traditional questionnaire (income years through 2013; regular payments only); the redesigned questionnaire with legacy processing (split panel for income year 2013, full sample from income year 2014); and the redesigned questionnaire with updated processing, which counts withdrawals net of rollovers (Rothbaum's side-by-side comparisons cover income years 2016 and 2017; I did not confirm from a Census source the exact income year at which the official published series switches). A chart must show breaks at both points; the 2012 to 2014 jump in the SSA "private pensions or annuities" series (26.8% to 37.4%) is mostly the questionnaire.
- Even the current CPS should be read as a lower bound on the share of older people drawing on retirement accounts: 47% (survey) against 69% (records) in 2015.

### Gaps
- The annual CPS counts of people 65+ with IRA/Keogh/401(k) income (PINC-08, data years 1994 to 2025) could not be read (Excel only), so there is no year-by-year CPS series of retirement-account recipients in these notes.
- Dollar aggregates behind Bee and Mitchell's Table 1 and the year-by-year values of their Figure 5 (1995, 1998 to 2011) were not retrieved.
- The full text of Bee, Dushi, Mitchell and Trenkamp (2024) is hosted on a server that blocks automated reading; only the abstract is covered.
- An early Census evaluation (Bee 2013) concluded that "the CPS ASEC measures retirement income well" for in-scope regular income; one read returned a figure of about 91% of matched 1099-R recipients reporting retirement income, which conflicts with the 46% false-negative rate in Bee and Mitchell (2017). I could not reconcile the two populations and recommend citing only the 2017 paper. — [Bee 2013](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2013/demo/Bee-PAA-paper.html)
- No linked-record evaluation of the CPS after the 2019 processing change (income years 2018 onward) was found.

## 3. HRS: incidence and size of withdrawals by age, and asset drawdown in retirement

### Takeaway
HRS-based studies agree on a sharp age pattern: about a fifth to two-fifths of IRA-owning households withdraw in a given period before the required-distribution age, then about three-quarters or more after it, and average withdrawals are about 2% of balances a year before 70 and 4% to 6% after. Total non-housing assets fall slowly: retirees still hold about 80% of pre-retirement assets after 17 to 18 years and roughly a third have more than they started with. In income terms, 28.7% of people 65+ lived in households with IRA withdrawals in 2019 (median $8,945).

### Inferences
- HRS two-year incidence (22% at 61, 40.5% at 69, 77% at 71) and SIPP one-year incidence (a little over 10% at 60, about 25% at 69, over 60% at 71) describe the same step at the required-distribution age; differences in level are mostly the reference period.
- The 28.7% to 29.3% of people 65+ with household IRA withdrawals in the HRS (2017, 2019) sits well above anything the pre-redesign CPS recorded and close to the 29% with IRA distributions in Bee and Mitchell's 1099-R records for 2012, which suggests the HRS captures IRA withdrawals reasonably well once the withdrawal items are used.
- The asset-preservation findings concern total non-housing wealth, not retirement accounts alone; they are consistent with withdrawals being largely saved or offset by returns (Banerjee: 31.5% of households 71 to 80 moved the withdrawal into other savings).

### Gaps
- No HRS study using the 2020 or 2022 waves that reports the share of households withdrawing by single year of age was found; the newest by-age incidence is Banerjee (2013) and Poterba, Venti and Wise (data to 2010). Effects of the RMD age moving to 72 (2020) and 73 (2023) are therefore not visible in survey-based evidence here.
- Banerjee's average percent of balance withdrawn by age group (61 to 70, 71 to 80) and the exact HRS waves used were not retrievable; EBRI's full PDFs require a member login.
- The CRR briefs named in the brief as "How Much Do Retirees Draw Down?" and "Will Today's Retirees Draw Down Their 401(k)s?" were not found under those titles; the closest matches are the Wettstein and Siliciano brief above and Munnell's column "How Will Boomers Draw Down Their 401(k) Balances?" (3 May 2022, https://crr.bc.edu/?p=36166), which summarises the same study: "Recent retirees without defined benefit plans would draw down about a quarter of their wealth by age 70" and "At this rate, Boomers would deplete their assets by 85, even though roughly half would survive past this age."
- Love and Smith, Smith-Soto-Penner and Coile-Milligan were read at abstract or summary level; they contain no published withdrawal-rate tables that I could extract.

## 4. SIPP and CPS pension supplements: withdrawals by people 65+, and what recipients did with lump-sum distributions

### Takeaway
SIPP gives the only repeated survey snapshot of withdrawal-taking among people 65+: among account owners 47.8% took a distribution in 1998, 54.5% in 2006 and 44.3% in 2009 (the 2009 dip coincides with the one-year RMD suspension), with a stable gap between ages 65 to 69 (about 20%) and 70+ (59% to 72%). On lump sums, the share of recipients putting the entire distribution into tax-qualified savings rose from roughly one in ten (1988) and 19.3% (1993) to 35.4% (1998) and about 44% to 45% (2003 to 2012); recipients aged 55 to 64 roll over the most (about 55% to 58%), while those 65+ roll over less (about 39% to 45%).

### Cited Findings

Withdrawals by people 65+ in SIPP
- Persons 65+ owning an IRA or 401(k)-type account (SIPP 1996, 2004 and 2008 panels): 1998 24.3% (65 to 69 37.1%; 70+ 18.8%); 2006 34.6% (43.0%; 31.2%); 2009 37.4% (45.3%; 33.8%). (2x) — [Anguelov, Iams and Purcell 2012, Chart 1](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v72n3/v72n3p59.html)

### Inferences
- The rise in full rollovers happened mostly between distributions received before 1987 and those received 1987 to 1998, which lines up with the 1986 early-withdrawal penalty and the 1992 direct-rollover and mandatory-withholding rules (rule dates are from my general knowledge, not from the sources opened); since about 1998 the share rolling over everything has been flat at about 44% to 45%, and it slipped for distributions received 2007 to 2012.
- By the 2000s rollover had become all-or-nothing: 94% of those who rolled anything over rolled everything, against 46.5% in 1993, so "partial rollover" is now a small category in survey data.
- Recipients 65 and older consistently roll over less than those in their late 50s and early 60s (41.5% against 55.9% in 2012; 39.2% against 56.4% in 2003; 44.7% against 53.1% in 1998). At those ages a non-rollover is often the start of retirement spending rather than leakage, and the sources do not separate the two.
- The 2009 fall in SIPP withdrawal-taking among owners aged 70+ (72.4% to 59.2%) is most plausibly the one-year suspension of required distributions; tax data show a drop of similar size (from the 90% range to 60%).

### Gaps
- EBRI's April 1993 EBS issue brief (Yakoboski, 1994), the EBRI Notes for 2002 and 2005, and the DOL tabulations of the 1988 and 1993 supplements could not be opened; 1988 and 1993 figures come from Piacentini, Woods, Poterba-Venti-Wise and Copeland's retrospective figure. The three 1993 "full rollover" figures (19.3%, 21.6%, 23%) use different definitions and age limits.
- SIPP stopped fielding the lump-sum topical module after the 2008 panel; no comparable tabulation for distributions after 2012 was found in the redesigned SIPP.
- No Census Bureau report on lump-sum dispositions by age was found; the Census material located covers retirement income receipt only.
- Nothing published from SIPP after 2009 gives withdrawal-taking among account owners 65+ by age on the Anguelov-Iams-Purcell definition, so that series has three points (1998, 2006, 2009).

## 5. Survey measures against administrative totals

### Takeaway
The CPS is the outlier. On comparable definitions the SCF, SIPP and HRS capture 94% to 99% of administrative retirement plan income for people 65+, the PSID 85%, and the CPS 47% even after its redesign; before the redesign the CPS recorded $6.4 billion of IRA withdrawals for 2006 against $124.7 billion on tax returns. Administrative totals themselves need care, because gross Form 1099-R and Form 1040 "pensions and annuities" amounts include rollovers unless these are removed.

### Inferences
- The surveys that ask about balances and then about withdrawals from each account (SCF, SIPP, HRS) line up with tax totals; the survey that asks about income sources with a payment frequency (CPS) does not. Question design, not respondent knowledge, appears to be the main driver.
- SIPP looks very different in the two comparisons (27% of tax totals for 2009 in Iams and Purcell; 97% for 2014 in CRR). The 2014 SIPP redesign, the broader "retirement plan income" definition in CRR (which includes DB pensions) and the different tax benchmarks all contribute; the two numbers should not be put on one chart.
- Part of the gap in older comparisons is definitional rather than misreporting (irregular withdrawals were deliberately out of scope for the traditional CPS), which is why a Census author described it as "a difference in the respective scopes of the two data sources." None of the sources opened quantifies how much of the gap is definitional.

### Gaps
- No single source gives a year-by-year series of CPS (or SCF) aggregate retirement account withdrawals against IRS totals; available points are 2006, 2012, 2014 to 2017 and 2023, each on a different definition.
- Miller and Schieber's Journal of Retirement article and Czajka and Denmead (2011) were not opened; their figures appear here only as quoted by others.

### Inferences
- Any "latest" SCF number in the report must be labelled 2022; if the report is published after late 2026 the writer should check for the 2025 SCF release.
- Survey evidence on withdrawal behaviour by age largely predates the changes to the required-distribution age (72 from 2020, 73 from 2023) and the 2020 RMD waiver (rule dates from my general knowledge; the CRR brief 22-8 confirms the move to 72 came after the last HRS wave it used); the SIPP 2022 and HRS 2019 figures above are the only post-2017 points and none isolates those rule changes.

- The by-age detail of the 2026 and 2025 CPS ASEC (PINC-08) and the SIPP 2024 wealth tables could not be read (Excel, or PDF on a server that blocks automated access).
- The 2025 and 2026 SHED retirement chapters could not be opened (page not found at the guessed addresses).

## Gaps and caveats

Method and reliability
- Every figure was obtained through an automated page reader that summarises documents; I could not open files directly. Multi-column PDF tables are the main risk. For the Federal Reserve Bulletin tables I had the reader print raw rows and took the seventh data column ("Retirement accounts"); the implied 65+ averages agree with the independent SSA tabulation, but the values should be checked against the Fed's historic tables before publication. Items marked 1x deserve the same check. Two misreads were caught only on re-reading (the columns of Banerjee's Figure 1 on DC plan disposition, and the column labels of Poterba-Venti-Wise's 1993 rollover table), which shows the size of the risk for single-read tables.
- The session's web search allowance ran out before the work was finished. After that point I could only open documents at addresses already known, which is why some named studies are covered from abstracts or trade-press summaries.

Documents that could not be opened
- Federal Reserve SCF historic tables (Excel) and chartbook data (CSV): unreadable binary. Consequence: no single-vintage by-age SCF series; 1989, 2010 and 2016 by age are missing; 2013, 2019 and 2022 by age come from GAO and consumer-finance websites and are rounded.
- SCF 2022 codebook: too long for the reader to reach the variable definitions; question wording comes from the questionnaire outline and the Bulletin macro instead.
- Census PINC-08 tables and all files on the Census "www2" server (including the full Bee-Dushi-Mitchell-Trenkamp 2024 paper, the P60-289 PDF and the SIPP wealth report PDF): blocked or Excel only.
- EBRI full-text PDFs (Banerjee 2013 EBRI Notes, EBRI Issue Brief 447, Copeland's SCF issue briefs, EBRI Notes January 2009, the 1994 issue brief on the April 1993 EBS): member login required. The November 2013 EBRI Notes was read from a copy hosted by SHRM; the BlackRock/EBRI paper from a copy hosted by the Insured Retirement Institute (BlackRock's own link returns an error page).
- Not located at all: Miller and Schieber (Journal of Retirement), Czajka and Denmead (2011), Johnson-Burman-Kobes (Urban Institute, 2004), Hurd and Panis (2006), any Federal Reserve FEDS Note on retiree withdrawals, the CRR briefs under the exact titles given in the brief, and EBRI's "Withdrawal Activity" notes (those use the EBRI IRA Database, which is administrative rather than a household survey).

Definitional traps the writer should keep in view
- Reference periods differ: SIPP incidence is annual (built from monthly reports), HRS incidence covers about two years since the last interview, SCF and redesigned CPS ask about the previous calendar year. HRS incidence is therefore higher than SIPP at the same age.
- Units differ: SCF families/households by age of head; SSA "aged units"; CPS and SIPP persons; HRS households or individuals with household-level income. Shares are not interchangeable across units.
- "Pension income" in CPS-based series (SSA, EBRI, Pension Rights Center) mixes DB pensions, annuities and retirement account payments; only PINC-08 separates IRA/Keogh/401(k) income and that table was unreadable here.
- CPS has two breaks: the questionnaire redesign (income year 2013 split panel; all of the sample from income year 2014) and the processing update (legacy and updated estimates published side by side for income years 2016 and 2017; exact switch year of the official series not confirmed here). The SSA series ends in 2014.
- SCF withdrawal data are usable only from the 2004 survey onward.
- SIPP changed who is asked about withdrawals in the 2022 survey (all account owners rather than only the ever-retired), and its lump-sum topical module ended with the 2008 panel.
- 2009 was a year without required minimum distributions, which depresses every withdrawal-incidence figure for that year (SIPP 2009 owners aged 70+: 59.2% against 72.4% in 2006).
- Lump-sum studies define "rollover" differently (entire amount to tax-qualified savings; any portion; probability models; samples limited to recipients under 60). Table AA puts them side by side for the age pattern only; levels across rows are not a time series. Table Y is the one consistent time series.
- At ages 65+ the surveys do not separate a lump sum taken to spend in retirement from a cashout in the leakage sense; "full cashout" for retirees is observed only as "withdrew the money" in the HRS disposition questions.
- Administrative benchmarks are not uniform: taxable IRA distributions on Form 1040 ($124.7 billion in 2006), total IRA distributions including nontaxable amounts, Form 1099-R gross distributions with direct rollovers removed (Bee and Mitchell), and SOI retirement plan income for filers 65+ (CRR) are four different yardsticks.

Conflicts between sources (unresolved)
- Poterba, Venti and Wise: the abstract says 7% of PRA-owning households aged 60 to 69 withdraw more than 10% of the balance and "about five percent" a year is withdrawn after 70½; the text says 8% and 5.8%.
- Bee and Mitchell: survey receipt for 2012 is given as 37% in one passage and 36% in another.
- April 1993 CPS supplement full-rollover share: 19.3% (EBRI, entire amount to tax-qualified savings), 21.6% (Poterba-Venti-Wise strict definition), 23% (CRS, recipients under 60).
- The $529 billion tax-data benchmark for people over 60 is dated 2007 in one SSA article and 2006 in my read of the other.
- SIPP's coverage of tax-reported distributions is put at about 27% (2009, Iams and Purcell) and 97% (2014, CRR) on different definitions and survey designs.
- The Census fact sheet for 2022 gives 48% of adults 65+ with retirement income; the reader also returned "41% (23.4 million of 58 million)" for the three listed sources combined. I could not confirm the second figure and have not used it.
- Bee (2013) concluded the CPS "measures retirement income well"; Bee and Mitchell (2017) found a 46% false-negative rate. The later, larger study should govern.
